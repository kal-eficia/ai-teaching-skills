# Year 10 electricity quiz: what went wrong and what to do

**Assumptions:** Q8 is a "what does a battery store" question with chemical energy as the key. Q5 assumes the bulbs are identical and each branch is connected straight across the same battery. I've used no student names, so everything below is by count only.

## Short version

Most of the errors come from **two ideas**, not five:

1. **Current is a fuel that gets used up** (Q3, and probably Q5).
2. **A battery is a tank of electricity** (Q8, and probably Q5).

Q6 and Q9 are mostly small mix-ups between similar terms, and they matter less.

---

## 1. "Current gets used up as it goes round" (fix first)

- **The wrong idea:** "The bulb uses up the current, so there's less after it."
- **Evidence:** Q3. 14 of 26 (54%) said current gets smaller or is used up, and 9 of those 14 were very confident. This is the biggest and most confident group on the quiz.
- **Why it's appealing:** In everyday language, things that "work" get used up, like petrol, phone charge and food. The bulb lights, so something must have been spent. Students also mix up current (what flows) with energy (what gets transferred). It is the energy that is used up, not the current.
- **The fix:**
  - **Counter-example:** Put an ammeter on each side of one bulb in a series circuit. Before the demo, ask students to predict the second reading and write it down. The readings match. A second ammeter after another bulb also matches.
  - **Contrast:** Current is the flow of charge, and it is the same all the way round a loop. Energy is what the charge carries from the battery to the bulb. The charge goes back to the battery, and the energy is left behind as light and heat.
  - **5-minute reteach:**
    1. (1 min) Students predict the second ammeter reading.
    2. (1 min) Show the demo or a simulation.
    3. (2 min) Use a model they can act out. Students walk in a loop holding a bag of "energy tokens". They hand the tokens to the bulb station and carry on walking with an empty bag. The number of walkers passing each point per minute is the same everywhere. A loop of bike chain or a pipe full of water works too.
    4. (1 min) Students finish the sentence: "The ______ is used up, the ______ is not."
  - **Hinge question (check it's gone):** *In a series circuit, ammeter 1 reads 0.4 A just before a bulb. What does ammeter 2 read just after the bulb?*
    - A) 0.2 A
    - B) 0 A
    - C) 0.4 A
    - D) 0.8 A

    Correct: C. A, B and D each match a wrong idea (halved, all used, doubled).
- **When to revisit:** The next two starters. The first should be a series-circuit prediction with a different component, such as a resistor. The second should ask students to explain, in one sentence, what is "used up" in a circuit.

---

## 2. "A battery stores electricity or charge" (high priority)

- **The wrong idea:** "A battery is a tank of electricity that pours out."
- **Evidence:** Q8. 12 of 26 (46%) said electricity or charge, and 8 of those 12 were confident.
- **Why it's appealing:** Everyday language says "charged", "flat", "out of juice" and "stores power". The word "charge" is also correct physics vocabulary, so students think they have answered the question right. In fact the charge is already in the wires and the bulb. The battery supplies **energy** to push it round.
- **Link to idea 1:** Both ideas treat electricity as a substance that gets emptied out of the battery and used up. If you fix both together, they support each other.
- **The fix:**
  - **Counter-example:** A battery has no charge to run out of. Charge flows around the loop, and the battery gives it energy. A battery can be "flat" while all the same charge is still in the wires.
  - **Contrast:** Chemical energy store in the battery, then electrical transfer, then light and heat in the bulb. Use an energy transfer chain, not a "tank".
  - **5-minute reteach:**
    1. (1 min) Show a cell with a dead-flat one beside it, with the same wires and bulb.
    2. (2 min) Students draw the energy transfer chain in a circuit, with the battery labelled as a chemical energy store.
    3. (2 min) Students complete: "The battery does not store ______. It stores ______ and gives it to the charge."
  - **Hinge question:** *Which statement about a battery in a working circuit is correct?*
    - A) It stores energy, and the charge carries it round the circuit.
    - B) It stores the current, and it runs out when it is used up.
    - C) It stores electrons, which come out of one end.
    - D) It stores voltage, which the bulb drains away.

    Correct: A. To avoid a guessable pattern, change the position of the correct option each time you reuse this question.
- **When to revisit:** The next two starters. The first is an energy transfer chain for a different device (such as a torch or a buzzer). The second is a "which words are wrong?" starter with sentences that include "stores current" and "electricity runs out".

## 3. "More bulbs in parallel means each is dimmer" (needs a quick check)

- **The wrong idea:** "The battery has a fixed amount to share, so two bulbs get less each."
- **Evidence:** Q5. 11 of 26 (42%) said dimmer, and most of them were *not* confident. They are guessing at a rule, not holding onto a belief, so this should be easy to fix.
- **Why it's appealing:** Two possible causes:
  - **Sharing model:** "The battery has a fixed supply." This comes straight from ideas 1 and 2.
  - **Series/parallel mix-up:** In series, adding bulbs really does make each dimmer, and students apply that rule to parallel.

  The quiz can't tell these apart. **Ask a few students why** they chose "dimmer", or look at their working. The answer decides whether you need the sharing model or the series/parallel contrast.
- **The fix:**
  - **Counter-example:** Build one bulb, then add a second bulb in parallel. Brightness doesn't change. Then make the second bulb in series and show the dimming. The side-by-side contrast does most of the work.
  - **Contrast:** Each parallel branch connects straight across the battery, so each bulb gets the full battery push. The battery supplies more total current, which is why batteries drain faster with more branches.
  - **5-minute reteach:**
    1. (1 min) Predict-observe-explain with one bulb versus two in parallel.
    2. (2 min) Redraw the circuit as two separate loops, each sharing the battery.
    3. (2 min) Students write one sentence: "In parallel each bulb is as bright as it would be ______, but the battery ______."
  - **Hinge question:** *A battery lights one bulb. You add a second identical bulb in parallel. What happens to the brightness of the first bulb?*
    - A) It gets dimmer, because the current is shared.
    - B) It goes out, because the current goes to the other bulb.
    - C) It gets brighter, because there are two paths.
    - D) It stays the same, because it is still connected straight across the battery.

    Correct: D. A and B test the sharing model, and C tests the idea that more paths means more brightness.
- **When to revisit:** The next two starters. The first is a series versus parallel brightness contrast. The second is a "battery lasts longer or shorter?" question, which tests the idea that batteries drain faster with more branches.

## 4. Voltmeter and ammeter mix-up (small group, partly a slip)

- **The wrong idea:** "A voltmeter measures current."
- **Evidence:** Q6. 4 of 26 said current, and 3 left it blank. Confidence data for these wasn't given. 4 students is a small group, and blank answers suggest uncertainty rather than a firm wrong belief.
- **Why it's appealing:** Both are "meters in a circuit", and the names sound alike. If students don't know how each meter is connected (series versus parallel), the two jobs blur together.
- **The fix:**
  - **Contrast:** Ammeter in series (measures flow past a point). Voltmeter across a component (measures the energy transferred to each unit of charge).
  - **5-minute reteach:** Give out a circuit diagram. Students colour the ammeter and the voltmeter in two colours and write one sentence on each using "in series" and "across".
  - **Hinge question:** *You want to measure how much energy each unit of charge transfers to a bulb. How should you connect the meter?*
    - A) A voltmeter in series with the bulb.
    - B) An ammeter in parallel with the bulb.
    - C) An ammeter in series with the bulb.
    - D) A voltmeter in parallel with the bulb.

    Correct: D.
- **When to revisit:** The next two starters. The first is a circuit symbol recall (ammeter versus voltmeter, and how each connects). The second is "which meter, and where?" for a new circuit.
- **Note:** The 3 blanks should be followed up with those students. They may simply not know, which is a gap, not a misconception.

---

## 5. Unit slip (probably not worth class time)

- **The wrong idea:** "The unit of resistance is amps (or volts)."
- **Evidence:** Q9. 2 wrote amps and 1 wrote volts. Three students, low numbers, no confidence data. This looks like a **slip or a recall gap**, not a shared misconception.
- **The fix:** Add the units to the next retrieval starter (current A, voltage V, resistance Ω) and have those three students check themselves. No full reteach is needed.
- **When to revisit:** Fold it into the same two starters as idea 4, as a quick "name the unit" row.

---

## What to do, in order

1. **Next lesson (about 10 minutes):** Reteach ideas 1 and 2 together with one demo and one energy transfer chain. This is where the confident errors are.
2. **Same lesson (about 5 minutes):** Run the Q5 predict-observe-explain. Ask a few students why they chose "dimmer", so you know which cause you're dealing with.
3. **Starters over the next two weeks:** Cover the revisit items above.
4. **After about a week:** Re-ask the three hinge questions on ideas 1 to 3. Mix up the correct-answer positions, and keep the same wrong ideas as distractors.
5. **Q6 and Q9:** Quick follow-up with the individuals involved, not a class lesson.

## One-line summary

**Fix first: "current gets used up" (Q3).** It was the largest group (14 of 26), most of them (9) were confident, and the battery-as-tank idea (Q8) and probably the Q5 error come from the same "electricity is a fuel" picture.
