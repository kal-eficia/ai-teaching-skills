# Year 10 electricity quiz: what the results show and what to do next

## Assumptions
- Your class has met current, voltage and resistance before (KS3 or earlier in Year 10), so these are persistent misconceptions and not gaps from missed teaching.
- You have a normal lesson, a few circuit components (cells, bulbs, ammeters, voltmeters, wires) and about 2 to 3 lessons or starters to spend on this.
- "Confident" means students gave a confidence rating alongside answers. I have used that to prioritise.

## Triage: where to spend your time

| Question | Misconception | Scale | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q3 | Current is used up by the bulb | 14/26 (54%), 9 very confident | Highest |
| Q8 | A battery stores charge/electricity | 12/26 (46%), 8 confident | High |
| Q5 | Parallel bulbs are each dimmer | 11/26 (42%), mostly unsure | Medium (quick fix) |
| Q6 | Voltmeter measures current | 4 wrong, 3 blank | Low-medium, small group |
| Q9 | Resistance unit wrong | 3/26 | Low, recall issue |

Confident wrong answers (Q3, Q8) are the dangerous ones. Those students will not question their answer, so telling them the right answer rarely works. Unsure wrong answers (Q5) are easier: those students are already open to being shown.

## Q3 and Q8 are probably one underlying idea

Q3 ("current is used up") and Q8 ("battery stores electricity/charge") both come from the same mental model: electricity is a *stuff* that the battery holds, the bulb consumes, and then it is gone. It is the most common electricity misconception at this age, and the language of everyday life ("the battery's run out of electricity", "power use", "electricity bill") reinforces it.

The fix is to replace it with a better model, not to patch each fact.

**The model to teach:**
- The charge (electrons in the wire) is already in the circuit, like water already in a closed loop of pipe. The battery does not store it and the bulb does not use it up.
- The battery is a pump. It stores *chemical energy* and uses it to push the charge round.
- The bulb transfers energy from the charge to the surroundings as light and heat. The charge keeps going round.
- So current is the same all the way round a series loop. Energy is what gets transferred.

### Step 1: Confront it with evidence (one lesson starter, 10 to 15 minutes)
Do a predict-observe-explain activity.
1. Show a series circuit: cell, ammeter A1, bulb, ammeter A2.
2. Ask students to predict, individually on mini-whiteboards: "Will A2 read less than, the same as, or more than A1?" Ask for a confidence rating too.
3. Reveal: the readings are the same. (Use a simulation such as PhET Circuit Construction Kit if you lack two ammeters.)
4. Ask: "If the bulb used up current, what would A2 show? So what is the bulb actually doing?"

Because 9 students were very confident, make them commit to a prediction in writing first. The surprise when they are wrong is what makes the correction stick.

### Step 2: Give them an analogy, then its limits
Use a **closed loop of rope** or a **bicycle chain** (pupils each hold a rope and pull it round; the pushing person is the battery, a pupil with a rough glove is the bulb). Everyone moves at the same rate, the rope is never used up, but the pushing person gets tired and the glove gets hot. Say clearly: the rope = charge, the tiredness = chemical energy running down, the heat = energy transferred.

Warn about the limit: no analogy is perfect, so tell them what it gets right (same current everywhere in series, energy transferred not charge) and where it breaks.

### Step 3: Fix the Q8 wording directly
- Write on the board: **"A battery stores chemical energy, not electricity or charge."**
- Show a dead battery vs a new one. Ask: "What is different? Not the number of electrons in the wires. The chemical store is depleted."
- Ask students to rewrite the quiz answer in their own words with the sentence stem: "The battery does not store ___, it stores ___ which it uses to ___."

### Step 4: Retrieval over the next two weeks
Misconceptions return. Put one low-stakes question in a starter after 2 days, 1 week and 3 weeks, for example:
- "A 1 A current enters a bulb. What is the current leaving it? Explain in one sentence."
- "Where is the energy stored in a torch before you switch it on?"

## Q5: Identical bulbs in parallel (11 said dimmer)

Most were not confident, so this is a gap in understanding, not a firmly held wrong idea. It most likely comes from applying series reasoning ("more bulbs, shared supply, dimmer") to a parallel circuit.

**Do this (15 minutes):**
- Build or simulate: one bulb on a cell, then two identical bulbs in series, then two in parallel. Have students predict brightness of each bulb in each case before you show them.
- Key idea to make explicit: **each parallel branch is connected directly across the battery, so each gets the full battery voltage**, so each bulb is as bright as the single bulb. The battery supplies more total current, so it drains faster.
- Contrast with series: the voltage is shared, so each bulb is dimmer.

A useful summary table for students to complete themselves:

| | Series (2 bulbs) | Parallel (2 bulbs) |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage across each bulb | | |
| Current through each bulb | | |
| Brightness of each bulb | | |
| Total current from the cell | | |

If the class built the series model for Q3 properly, this follows from it.

## Q6: What does a voltmeter measure? (4 said current, 3 blank)

Seven students (27%) do not have a secure idea of what a voltmeter is for. It is a smaller issue, but it will block later work on potential difference, so fix it before moving on.

- Give a two-line anchor: **Ammeter = current, in series (it is in the loop). Voltmeter = potential difference, in parallel (it is across a component).**
- Quick practical or simulation: place both meters in a circuit, move them, and see what happens (an ammeter placed in parallel gives a surprising result in a sim; a voltmeter in series gives near-zero current).
- Have the 7 students do a short targeted task: label meter symbols on three circuit diagrams and state what each measures.
- The three who left it blank are worth a quiet word. Blank often means "I do not know and I am worried about being wrong", not a misconception.

## Q9: Unit of resistance (2 said amps, 1 said volts)

Three students, and the pattern looks like a recall mix-up of the three units (amps, volts, ohms), not a conceptual issue. Handle it lightly:
- Use a triangle or table linking quantity, symbol and unit: current (I, ampere, A); potential difference (V, volt, V); resistance (R, ohm, Ω).
- Include these in your retrieval starters. Quick quiz, no discussion needed.
- If they mix these up on calculation questions too (V = IR), check their working.

## Suggested sequence

| When | What | Targets |
|---|---|---|
| Next lesson, starter | Predict-observe-explain on series current, then battery stores energy | Q3, Q8 |
| Same lesson, main | Parallel vs series brightness investigation and table | Q5 |
| Following lesson, starter | Meters and units mini-task | Q6, Q9 |
| +2 days, +1 week, +3 weeks | One retrieval question each time | All |
| After 3 weeks | Re-test the same five ideas with different wording | Check it worked |

## Two further checks

1. **Look at the wrong answers for Q8 more closely.** "Electricity or charge" is partly right in wording: a capacitor does store charge, and everyday speech says "stored electricity". Decide how strictly you are marking it. If your course (for example AQA or Edexcel GCSE) expects "chemical energy", then teach that the *cell* stores energy in chemical stores. That is not the same as storing charge.
2. **Check your own question wording.** For Q3, the phrase "after it passes through" can pull students towards "gets used up". Consider rephrasing in the re-test as "compare the current before and after the bulb". If a clearly worded version gets different results, you have learned something useful about which students actually hold the misconception.

## Summary
- Q3 and Q8 are the big ones and share a root: the idea that electricity is a substance stored and used up. Replace the model, do not just correct the answers.
- Use prediction first, then demonstration, so the confident students experience being wrong.
- Q5 is an easy win with a short practical on series versus parallel.
- Q6 and Q9 are small-group or retrieval fixes.
- Re-test in about three weeks to see whether the correction held.
