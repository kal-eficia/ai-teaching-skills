# Adapting the Year 8 Civil War extended answer

**Assumptions:** the task stays the same for the whole class. The three students keep the same learning goal (weigh Charles I against other causes, reach a judgement) but get different routes to it. I've assumed they have no TA, and that you know these students well, so treat anything below as a menu and drop what doesn't suit them.

## Changes that help everyone (do these first)

Doing these for the whole class means the three students aren't singled out.

1. **Break the 30 minutes into timed stages.** Put it on the board and use a visible timer.
   - 5 min: read the sources and annotate
   - 5 min: plan (see the grid below)
   - 15 min: write three PEEL paragraphs
   - 3 min: write the judgement
   - 2 min: check
2. **Give a planning grid** instead of a blank page. Rows are "Charles I", "Parliament and religion", "Money and taxes" (or whatever fits your three sources). Columns are "Evidence (source or own knowledge)" and "Does this show Charles was to blame? Why?"
3. **Provide a PEEL scaffold** with a model paragraph on a different topic (for example, the Gunpowder Plot or the Peasants' Revolt). Include the labels Point, Evidence, Explain, Link.
4. **Give a judgement stem bank:** "I agree to a large extent because...", "However, the most important factor was...", "Overall, Charles was / was not mainly to blame because...".
5. **State what a good answer looks like:** "3 paragraphs plus a conclusion, about 250 to 300 words." Say that length is not what earns the marks.

## One student with dyslexia

- **Reading load:** the sources on pages 42 to 44 are the main barrier. Provide them in advance (the night before if possible) or as a printout on a cream or pastel background, in a sans-serif font (Arial, Verdana, Calibri) at 12 to 14pt, with 1.5 line spacing, left-aligned and not justified. If you have a reading pen or text-to-speech, allow it. Alternatively, pair-read or read the sources aloud to the class first, which helps everyone.
- **Pre-highlight** key phrases in each source, or provide a one-line gloss of the hard vocabulary (for example, "ship money", "Parliament", "Puritan").
- **Writing load:** allow a laptop or speech-to-text if that is their usual way of working. Do not mark down spelling. Give them a word mat of key terms with correct spellings: Charles I, Parliament, divine right, Puritan, tax, rebellion, Scotland, Laud.
- **Reduce the amount to write, not the thinking.** Accept bullet points for the plan, and agree a lower target (for example, two full PEEL paragraphs plus a short conclusion) in advance with them privately.
- **Extra time:** 25% extra if that is their agreed arrangement, otherwise allow the stages to run slightly longer, or let them finish the conclusion for homework.
- **Marking:** mark on historical reasoning (the quality of the argument and use of evidence), not on spelling and punctuation, and tell them you are doing so.

## One student with ADHD

- **Chunk the task** into the timed stages above and give them their own **checklist with tick boxes** (Read, Annotate, Plan, Para 1, Para 2, Para 3, Conclusion, Check). Ticking each box gives frequent small wins.
- **Check in at each stage change** (a 10-second look at their plan, not a lecture). The planning stage is where they are most likely to drift, so look there first.
- **Reduce the starting barrier:** give them the first sentence of Paragraph 1 or a sentence starter for each paragraph so they are not staring at a blank page.
- **Seat them** away from the door and the window, near you, and with a clear desk. Allow a fidget item if it doesn't distract others.
- **Movement:** offer a legitimate job before the writing starts (hand out the sources, collect the sheets), or a 60-second stand-and-stretch at the halfway point for the whole class.
- **Make the finish line visible:** a timer they can see, and "when you've done X you're done" for each stage. If the 30 minutes is a long stretch for them, allow them to split it into two shorter blocks.
- **Praise specific effort** ("You got your evidence in that paragraph") rather than general "focus".

## One autistic student who is very anxious about open-ended tasks

The question "How far do you agree?" is wide open, and that is the cause of the anxiety. The aim is to make it predictable and bounded without removing the thinking.

- **Preview it in advance.** Tell them today or tonight what tomorrow's task is, how long it is, and what it looks like (their copy of the worksheet, the question, the timings). Surprise is often the biggest trigger. Ideally send it to them or a parent or carer.
- **Narrow the question into concrete steps.** For example:
  1. Find two things in the sources that Charles I did.
  2. Find one thing someone else did (Parliament, Scots, religion, money).
  3. Decide which matters more, and give one reason.
  
  A step-by-step checklist like this turns "How far do you agree?" into a series of closed questions.
- **Explicit success criteria:** "A good answer has: 3 paragraphs, each with a Point, a piece of Evidence, an Explanation and a Link; a conclusion that says 'I agree / partly agree / disagree' and gives a reason." Say it is acceptable to have a view you are not 100% sure of.
- **Give a fixed structure and a worked example** so there is no guessing about what is wanted. Avoid vague phrases like "write as much as you can".
- **Offer a choice of defined options** instead of an open one. For example, "Pick one: (a) I mostly agree, (b) I partly agree, (c) I mostly disagree", with a stem for each. This takes the decision about what to argue off them, while they still choose the position.
- **Literal language:** avoid idioms in the instructions. "Reach a judgement" may be unclear, so say "Write your final answer to the question, and give your reason."
- **Predictable routine and environment:** the same seat, a quiet corner if they need it, and agree in advance how they can signal if they're overwhelmed (a card on the desk, a quiet word, permission to step out for two minutes) without having to explain in front of the class.
- **Reassure on "right answers":** say clearly that there is no single correct answer in history and that you are marking how well they support their view. Many autistic students worry they will pick the wrong side.

## Practical summary for tomorrow

| Student | Key adaptations |
|---|---|
| Dyslexia | Sources in advance, pre-highlighted, readable format, word mat, laptop or speech-to-text, extra time, mark on content not spelling |
| ADHD | Timed stages, tick-box checklist, sentence starter, check-ins at stage changes, seating, movement job |
| Autism and anxiety | Advance preview, narrowed step-by-step prompts, explicit success criteria, choice of defined positions, literal wording, agreed signal for breaks |

## Preparation checklist (about 20 to 30 minutes)

- Make one **planning grid** and one **PEEL model paragraph** (everyone gets these).
- Print the **stage checklist** with timings.
- Make a **word mat** and a **judgement stem bank** (one A5 card).
- Prepare **enlarged, pre-highlighted copies** of pages 42 to 44 for the student with dyslexia.
- Make a **short, step-by-step version of the question** for the autistic student, and send or show it in advance.
- Check any access arrangements already in the students' support plans (SEN support or EHCP) and follow those first. They override anything above.
