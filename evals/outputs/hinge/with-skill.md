# Hinge questions: adding fractions with different denominators (Year 8)

Three options. Pick **one** to ask mid-lesson. Each takes under a minute and every wrong answer tells you what the student is thinking.

**How to run it:** show the question, give 30 seconds of silent thinking, then everyone answers at once (mini whiteboards, ABCD cards, fingers or a poll). Count the spread before you say anything.

**Assumptions:** the class has seen finding a common denominator and has done at least a couple of examples. Option 2 is the best choice if you've only just taught the method. Option 3 is best once they're comfortable and you want to know whether to move on to harder questions.

---

## Option 1: The whole calculation (quickest check)

**Question:** What is 1/2 + 1/3?

- A) 2/5
- B) 2/6
- C) 5/6
- D) 1/6

**Correct answer:** C

| Option | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| A) 2/5 | Adds the tops and adds the bottoms (1+1, 2+3). Thinks fractions add like whole numbers. |
| B) 2/6 | Adds the tops, multiplies the bottoms. Has the idea that denominators need changing, but not why. |
| D) 1/6 | Multiplies the fractions (1/2 × 1/3). Mixes up adding with multiplying. |

**Right for the wrong reason?** Unlikely. None of the three misconceptions lands on 5/6 by accident.

**Decision rule:**
- **80% or more choose C:** move on to unlike denominators where one isn't a multiple of the other, such as 3/4 + 1/6.
- **Many choose A:** go back. Draw 1/2 and 1/3 of the same bar. Ask "is the answer 2/5 of a bar, when 1/2 alone is already more than 2/5?" The size check breaks the rule.
- **Many choose B:** the idea of a common denominator is missing. Re-teach why the pieces must be the same size before you can count them.
- **Many choose D:** quick contrast: "Which operation did you just do?" Then side by side: 1/2 of a pizza plus 1/3 of a pizza, against 1/2 of 1/3 of a pizza.
- **Split across several options:** don't reteach to everyone. Go to the bar model with the whole class for 5 minutes, then ask a fresh question.

---

## Option 2: The method (best right after teaching it)

**Question:** Which one shows 3/4 + 1/6 correctly rewritten with a common denominator, ready to add?

- A) 9/12 + 2/12
- B) 3/12 + 1/12
- C) 3/24 + 1/24
- D) 9/12 + 1/12

**Correct answer:** A

| Option | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| B) 3/12 + 1/12 | Changes the bottoms to 12 but leaves the tops alone. Doesn't see that the top must change too. |
| C) 3/24 + 1/24 | Multiplies the denominators (4 × 6) to get a common one, then forgets to scale the tops. Two errors at once. |
| D) 9/12 + 1/12 | Converts the first fraction correctly, then doesn't convert the second. A slip with the method rather than a deep gap. |

**Right for the wrong reason?** No. Only a student who scales both numerator and denominator on both fractions gets A.

**Decision rule:**
- **80% or more choose A:** move on to adding and simplifying the answer.
- **Many choose B:** go back to equivalent fractions for 5 minutes. "1/2 = ?/4. What happened to the top?" The rule: whatever you do to the bottom, you do to the top.
- **Many choose C:** they're using a common denominator that works, but not the smallest. That's fine to keep, as long as the tops are scaled. Show 3/24 is not the same size as 3/4 and fix the scaling. Look at lowest common multiples later.
- **Many choose D:** the method is mostly there. Don't reteach. Give them a "check each fraction" routine: tick the first, tick the second.
- **Roughly equal B and C:** equivalent fractions is the weak point, so reteach that before any more adding.

## Option 3: The harder case (deciding whether to extend)

**Question:** What is 2/3 + 3/4?

- A) 5/7
- B) 5/12
- C) 17/24
- D) 17/12

**Correct answer:** D (accept 1 5/12 if a student writes it that way. Tell the class you'll accept either form before they answer, so nobody gets caught on format.)

| Option | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| A) 5/7 | Adds tops and adds bottoms. The most basic misconception. |
| B) 5/12 | Finds the common denominator 12 correctly but adds the original tops (2+3) instead of converting them first. |
| C) 17/24 | Converts correctly to 8/12 + 9/12 and adds the tops to get 17, then adds the denominators (12+12). The last step is the problem. |

**Right for the wrong reason?** Very unlikely. Each wrong path has its own answer.

**Decision rule:**
- **80% or more choose D:** they're secure. Move on to subtraction, mixed numbers or word problems.
- **Many choose A:** they've missed the main idea. Go back to the bar model and use Option 1 as a follow-up.
- **Many choose B:** common denominator found but not applied. Practise "convert the tops" with two or three more examples on mini-whiteboards.
- **Many choose C:** very close. Ask "When we add 8 twelfths and 9 twelfths, how many twelfths do we have?" Then ask why the denominator stays as it is. A quick fix, then continue.
- **Many choose D but only after a long time:** they may be right but slow. Next step is fluency practice, not reteaching.

---

## Answer key check

The correct answers across the three questions are C, A, D. Checked with the no-answer-patterns script: clean (no repeats, runs, staircases or lopsidedness). If you use more than one option in the same lesson, ask them in the order above or any other order, and shuffle the answer positions again if you add more questions to the set.

## Which should you choose?

- Just finished teaching the method: **Option 2**.
- Want a fast yes/no on whether they've got it: **Option 1**.
- Class seems confident and you want to know whether to push on: **Option 3**.
