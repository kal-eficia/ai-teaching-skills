# Mid-lesson check: adding fractions with different denominators (Year 8)

**Assumptions:** the class has already seen equivalent fractions and finding a common denominator. You want a one-question, multiple-choice check (mini-whiteboards, ABCD cards or a show of hands) that takes about 60 seconds. Each wrong answer is built from a real misconception, so the pattern of responses tells you what to do next.

---

## Option 1: Quick procedural check (fastest, most common error)

**What is 1/2 + 1/3?**

- A) 2/5
- B) 2/6
- C) 5/6
- D) 1/5

**Correct answer: C**

| Answer | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| A (2/5) | Added tops and added bottoms. Core misconception, go back. |
| B (2/6) | Added tops, kept a denominator. Doesn't see why a common denominator is needed. |
| D (1/5) | Added the denominators and kept a numerator. Guessing, or no model of fractions. |
| C (5/6) | Secure on the basic method. |

**Decision:** If 80% or more choose C, move on. If more than 20% choose A, B or D, stop and re-teach with a visual (fraction wall or bar model) before any more practice.

---

## Option 2: Stretch check (tests whether the method holds up when one denominator isn't a multiple of the other)

**What is 3/4 + 1/6?**

- A) 11/12
- B) 4/10
- C) 4/24
- D) 10/12

**Correct answer: A**

| Answer | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| B (4/10) | Added tops and bottoms. Same core misconception as above. |
| C (4/24) | Multiplied the denominators but added the tops without scaling them. Knows a common denominator is needed but not that the numerators must change too. |
| D (10/12) | Found 12 correctly and converted 3/4 to 9/12, but left 1/6 as 1/12 instead of 2/12. Method is nearly there, slip in the second conversion. |
| A (11/12) | Secure, including when neither denominator divides the other. |

**Decision:** Mostly A, move on. Mostly C or D, go back to "whatever you do to the bottom, you do to the top" with two worked examples, then re-ask. A few B answers mean those students need a short 1-to-1 or small-group catch-up while the others start practice.

---

## Option 3: Understanding check (catches students who have memorised steps without meaning)

**Zain says 2/3 + 3/4 = 5/7. Without doing the full calculation, how can you tell he is wrong?**

- A) The denominators should have been added too, so it should be 5/7 plus something
- B) The answer is smaller than 3/4, but he has added something to 3/4, so it should be bigger
- C) 7 is a prime number, so it can't be a denominator
- D) You must always convert fractions to decimals first

**Correct answer: B**

| Answer | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| A | Confused about the rule itself. Needs the method re-taught. |
| C | Reaching for a surface rule rather than reasoning about size. |
| D | Thinks there is only one way to do it, or is guessing. |
| B | Can estimate and sense-check, so understands what the fractions mean. |

(5/7 is about 0.71 and 3/4 is 0.75, so Zain's answer is less than one of the things he started with. Students who spot this are ready to move on and may be ready for a harder task.)

**Decision:** Mostly B, move on and consider an extension. Mixed answers, spend two minutes on estimating ("is the answer bigger or smaller than 3/4, and why?") then revisit the method.

---

## Which should you pick?

- **Not sure the class has the basics at all:** Option 1.
- **You've already modelled one or two examples and want to know if they can do it independently:** Option 2. This is the best all-rounder.
- **Class seems confident and you want to check it isn't just procedure-following:** Option 3.

## Running it well

1. Give silent think time (30 seconds), then have everyone show their answer at the same moment so they don't copy each other.
2. Scan the room and tally roughly before you say anything.
3. Don't reveal the answer straight away. Ask a student who chose a wrong option to explain their thinking first, because the explanation is usually the most useful diagnostic you'll get.
4. Use the thresholds above as a guide, not a rule. If the students getting it wrong are a small, known group, move on and support them while the rest practise.
5. Shuffle the order of the options if you reuse any of these in another class, so students can't simply copy last period's answer position.
