# Using AI to Plan Lessons: 60-Minute Staff Meeting Session

**Audience:** 15 teachers, mixed subjects, mixed confidence, some sceptical, most have never used AI.
**Goal:** By the end, every teacher has used an AI tool on a real upcoming lesson, has seen where it goes wrong, and has committed to one specific thing to try in the next two weeks.

## Assumptions (please check these before the day)

- The school has an approved AI tool, or you will use a free general-purpose chatbot on your own login. Teachers need either their own device or to work in pairs on one device. Check wifi and log-ins the day before.
- The school has a data or safeguarding policy for AI. If it doesn't, use the default rules below and tell your SLT you've done so.
- Teachers pair up by subject or key stage where possible, and bring one real lesson they have to plan in the next two weeks. Send a short email the day before asking them to bring it (topic, year group, learning objective).
- You are comfortable using AI yourself. Rehearse the live demo once, with the exact prompts below, because AI output varies.

## Data rules to state at the start (30 seconds, put on a slide)

1. Never paste names, photos, SEN or safeguarding details, or anything that identifies a pupil.
2. No assessment data, reports or emails about individuals.
3. Planning content (topics, objectives, texts, exam specifications) is fine.
4. You are responsible for what you use. Check everything before it reaches a pupil.

If your school's policy differs, replace these with the school's wording.

## 0:00-0:05 Why this matters (5 min)

**Facilitator notes**
- Open with a question, not a pitch: "Hands up if you've planned a lesson on a Sunday evening this term." Then: "What would you do with an extra hour a week?"
- Name the sceptics early and kindly: "Some of you are sceptical, and some of that is well founded. AI gets things wrong, and it's not a replacement for your judgement. Today is about finding out for yourself, on your own lesson, whether it saves you time. If it doesn't, you've lost 25 minutes, and you'll know why."
- State the data rules (above).
- Set the one rule for the session: "You're the expert in the room. AI is a fast, sometimes sloppy assistant. You decide what stays."

## 0:05-0:17 The idea (12 min)

Three key points only. Put each on a slide with one example.

**1. AI is good at first drafts and variety, weak at accuracy and knowing your class.**
- Good for: lesson outlines, starter and plenary ideas, examples and analogies, questions at different levels, rewording text for different reading ages, differentiation ideas, checking a plan for gaps.
- Weak for: factual accuracy (it can state errors confidently, and invent sources or quotations), exam-board specifics, knowing your pupils, and anything that depends on what you taught last week.
- Say plainly: "It saves time on the blank page. It doesn't save you from checking."

**2. The quality of what you get depends on the context you give.**
Introduce a simple prompt recipe on a handout, with five slots:
- **Role and task:** "You are helping a secondary teacher plan a lesson."
- **Context:** year group, subject, topic, prior knowledge, lesson length
- **Objective:** what pupils should know or be able to do by the end
- **Constraints:** no tech, mixed ability, 50 minutes, specific misconceptions
- **Format:** a table with timings, a list of questions with answers, and so on

Compare a vague prompt ("Plan a lesson on fractions") with a rich one. The contrast does the persuading.

**3. Treat the first answer as a draft and iterate.**
- Follow-ups are where the value is: "Make the starter more active", "Add a worked example for the common error of adding denominators", "Cut this to 40 minutes", "Give me three exit-ticket questions with answers".
- Always read it as a subject expert: is it correct, is it pitched right, would it work with my class?

**Facilitator notes**
- Keep this moving. No jargon. Don't explain how language models work unless asked.
- If a sceptic raises concerns about cheating, jobs or the environment, acknowledge them briefly and park them: "That's a fair and bigger conversation. Can we have it at the end or in the follow-up? For the next 40 minutes I want to test whether it's useful for planning."

## 0:17-0:27 See it (10 min)

**Live demo on a screen, in two parts.**

**Part A, the vague prompt (2 min).** Type: *"Plan a lesson on the causes of the First World War."* (Swap in a topic from a subject that is well represented in the room, or ask a volunteer for a topic.) Show the generic result and ask: "Would you teach this? What's missing?"

**Part B, the rich prompt (4 min).** Type a prompt using the five slots, for example:

> You are helping a secondary history teacher. Plan a 50-minute lesson for a mixed-ability Year 9 class on the causes of the First World War. Pupils already know the alliance system but not the Balkan crisis. Objective: pupils can explain two causes of the July Crisis using evidence. No computers, one worksheet at most. Give a timed table with a starter, main activity, and exit ticket with three questions and model answers.

Then iterate once live: "Make the main task more discussion-based and add a scaffold for pupils who struggle with writing."

**Part C, show what it gets wrong (4 min).** This part matters most for the sceptics. Do one or more of:
- Ask it a factual question in your demo subject and highlight an error or an overconfident claim. (Rehearse beforehand and have a screenshot ready in case it behaves perfectly on the day.)
- Ask for a source or quotation and check whether it exists.
- Point out anything that wouldn't suit your actual class (pitch too high, unrealistic timings, American spelling or curriculum assumptions).

Say: "This is why you read everything. It's quicker to fix a good draft than to write from scratch, but only if you check it."

**Facilitator notes**
- Narrate your thinking as an expert reviewing the output: "That timing is unrealistic, I'd cut this." This models the habit you want.
- If the demo goes badly, that's useful; use it. Don't hide it.

## 0:27-0:50 Try it (23 min, protect this time)

**Task (on a handout and on screen):**

Working in pairs, take **one real lesson you need to plan in the next two weeks**.

1. **Prompt (5 min):** Write a prompt using the five slots. Run it.
2. **Improve (8 min):** Ask at least two follow-ups to improve it (pitch, differentiation, activity type, questions with answers, timing).
3. **Check (6 min):** Read the output as the subject expert. Mark up the page: keep (green), change (amber), wrong or unusable (red). Note any error you found.
4. **Decide (4 min):** What would you actually use? What did it save, and what didn't it do well?

**Pairing:**
- Pair a confident user with a beginner, or pair sceptics with each other and tell them their job is to find the flaws. Both approaches work. Sceptics who are given permission to critique tend to engage.
- If someone doesn't have a lesson, they can plan a revision lesson, a cover lesson or a starter sequence.

**Facilitator notes**
- Circulate. Don't take over keyboards. Ask: "What did you tell it about your class?" and "What would you change?"
- Common stuck points: prompts that are too short, accepting the first answer, and forgetting to check facts. Nudge, don't fix.
- Keep a mental note of two or three pairs with something worth sharing, including at least one that found a clear error (good for credibility) and one that saved real time.
- Remind pairs at 0:35 and 0:44 where they should be.

## 0:50-0:55 Share (5 min)

Pick two or three pairs. Each gets 90 seconds to answer:
- What did you ask for?
- What was useful?
- What did it get wrong or miss?

Give priority to at least one honest "it was rubbish at X" and one "this saved me real time." Don't force enthusiasm.

## 0:55-1:00 Commit (5 min)

**Commitment card** (print one per teacher, or use a shared form):

> **One thing I'll try in the next two weeks:**
> _______________________________________________
> **On which lesson or task:** _____________________
> **What I'll check before using the output:** ________
> **I'll tell [partner's name] how it went by:** _______

Teachers can also write "I'll try it and decide it's not for me", which is a legitimate commitment if they commit to actually testing it. Ask each pair to swap cards.

Close by confirming the follow-up (below) and collecting the cards, or taking a photo of them.

**Closing line:** "You don't have to love it. You just have to know, from your own experience, when it helps and when it doesn't."

## Handout outline (1 page, front and back)

**Front: Prompt recipe**
- The five slots with a fill-in template
- One vague prompt and one rich prompt side by side
- Ten useful follow-up phrases ("Make this harder for the top set", "Give common misconceptions", "Add a retrieval starter on last lesson's content", "Rewrite for a reading age of 11")

**Back: Check before you use it**
- Is it accurate? (Check against your textbook, spec or own knowledge)
- Is it pitched right for my class?
- Are the timings realistic?
- Are the sources, quotations and statistics real?
- Does it match our curriculum and exam board?
- Is it free of pupil data?
- Data rules summary

## Slides outline (7 to 9 slides)

1. Title and one-line goal
2. Data rules
3. The Sunday-night problem (and what we'll test today)
4. What AI is good at / weak at
5. The five-slot prompt recipe (vague vs rich example)
6. Iterate: example follow-up prompts
7. Try-it task and timings
8. Share prompts: "What did you ask? What helped? What went wrong?"
9. Commit and follow-up

## Follow-up plan (this is where the impact comes from)

| When | What | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Same day | Send the handout, the prompt template and the school data rules by email or shared drive | You |
| Day 3 to 5 | A short message in the staff channel: "Share one prompt that worked for you or one thing it got wrong" | You |
| Week 2 | **10-minute check-in** at the next department meeting or briefing: what did you try, what happened, what got in the way? Collect the commitment cards or ask for a show of hands | You |
| Week 2 to 4 | Optional peer observation or lesson-plan swap: a colleague looks at one AI-assisted plan and the teacher's edits (focus only on what was changed and why) | Volunteers |
| Week 4 | Build a shared bank of prompts that worked, sorted by subject and task, plus a "watch out" list of errors people found | You and volunteers |

**How to measure whether it worked:** how many teachers tried it on a real lesson; how many report saving time; which errors were found; whether anyone has made it a regular habit. Use this to decide whether to run a second session (for example, on resources, feedback or differentiation).

## Handling the sceptics

- Don't try to convert them. Ask them to test it and report honestly.
- Give them the "find the flaws" job. Their critical eye improves the whole room's practice.
- Validate real concerns (accuracy, bias, pupil data, academic integrity, workload creep) and say which ones the school is handling and which are open.
- Avoid hype and phrases like "this will change everything."

## Handling the beginners

- Provide the prompt template so nobody faces a blank box.
- Pair them with someone patient.
- Have a backup plan if log-in fails: a screen-share demo and a paper-based version of the prompt-writing task.

## Contingency

- **Tech fails:** do the Try-it task with the facilitator's screen, taking prompts from pairs and typing them live. You lose some hands-on time but keep the learning.
- **Running late:** trim the Idea section to two points (drop the second) and shorten Share to two pairs. Never cut Try it below 20 minutes.
- **Over-enthusiastic user dominates:** ask them to help a neighbour.
