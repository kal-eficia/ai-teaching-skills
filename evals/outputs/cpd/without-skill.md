# Staff meeting plan: Using AI to plan lessons (60 minutes, 15 teachers)

## Assumptions (change any that don't fit)
- Most staff have never used an AI chatbot. Some are sceptical, a few may be worried.
- Everyone has a laptop or can share one with a neighbour. Staff can reach a chatbot your school permits (for example Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Copilot). Check your school's acceptable use and data protection policy first, and use only the tool(s) it allows.
- You want people to leave having made something they will actually use, not a talk about AI.
- The session is in a room where people can sit in mixed-subject trios.

## Aim
By the end, every teacher has used AI to produce a draft of a real lesson (or part of one) for next week, has improved it with at least one follow-up prompt, and knows what to check before using anything it produces.

## Success criteria
- Every teacher has a usable draft for a real lesson.
- Every teacher can name two things to check in AI output and one thing never to paste into it.
- Sceptics have had their concerns heard and have seen a reason to try it once, with no pressure to adopt it.

## Before the session (about 45 minutes of prep)
1. **Check the policy.** Confirm which tools staff may use, whether staff need accounts, and what your data protection rules say. If there is no policy yet, say so in the session and use the "never paste" rules below as interim guidance.
2. **Get access sorted in advance.** Email staff the week before: bring a laptop, sign in to [approved tool] beforehand, and bring one lesson you have to plan in the next two weeks (topic, year group, any constraints). This removes the biggest time-waster, logins.
3. **Prepare a one-page handout** (see "Handout" below) and put it on a shared drive or print it.
4. **Test your live demo** on a topic in your own subject beforehand, and have a backup (screenshots or a saved chat) in case the wifi fails.
5. **Prepare one deliberately flawed output** to use in the "check it" section. Ask AI for something in a subject and find a real error, or use a known one (made-up reference, wrong date, an activity that wouldn't work in 50 minutes). Real errors are far more persuasive than warnings.

## Detailed run sheet

### 0:00 to 0:05 | Welcome and framing (5 min)
- **Open with the problem, not the technology:** "We all spend hours on planning. Let's see whether AI can give us some of that time back, and where it can't."
- **Quick poll** (hands up or sticky dots on a wall chart): Never used it / Tried once / Use it sometimes / Use it regularly. This shows the room that most people are beginners and normalises it.
- **Set ground rules:**
  - Nobody has to use it after today. The aim is an informed opinion.
  - Scepticism is welcome and useful. We'll use it in the session.
  - Today is about planning, not marking pupils' work or anything with pupil data.
- **State the one-sentence model** to keep it simple: *AI is like a fast, keen, slightly overconfident teaching assistant who has never met your class. It gives you a good first draft. You are still the teacher.*

### 0:05 to 0:15 | Live demo (10 min)
Do this on screen, in a subject that is not yours if possible, or one that's relatable to all (for example a Year 8 lesson on a topic in a core subject).

1. **Show a weak prompt first.** Type: "Plan a lesson on the water cycle." Show the generic result. Ask the room: "Would you use that?" (Usually, no.)
2. **Show a strong prompt.** Build it live using the recipe below: role, context, learners, constraints, format. For example:
   > You are an experienced secondary geography teacher. Plan a 50-minute Year 8 lesson on the water cycle. The class is mixed ability, 28 pupils, with 4 pupils who find reading difficult. They have already learned about weather and climate. I want a starter, a main activity with no more than 15 minutes of teacher talk, a short check for understanding, and a plenary. Use a table with timings. Avoid anything needing special equipment.
3. **Show iteration.** Follow up: "Make the main activity more hands-on," then "Add three differentiated questions: support, core, stretch," then "Write exit tickets with a model answer."
4. **Show a different type of task** to broaden the picture: ask it to turn a text into a vocabulary list with definitions, or create a retrieval quiz of 8 questions with answers.
5. **Narrate your judgement out loud:** "This bit is good, this bit is rubbish because..., I'd change this." Modelling critical evaluation is the most important thing you do all session.

### 0:15 to 0:20 | The prompt recipe and the "never paste" rules (5 min)
Show on one slide (and in the handout):

**Prompt recipe: R-C-L-C-F**
- **R**ole: "You are an experienced Year 9 science teacher."
- **C**ontext: topic, prior learning, where it fits in the unit.
- **L**earners: year group, class size, ability range, SEND/EAL needs described generally (no names).
- **C**onstraints: length, resources, what to avoid, exam board or curriculum.
- **F**ormat: table, bullet list, timings, differentiated tasks.

Then: "Don't accept the first answer. Ask it to change things. It's a conversation."

**Never paste in:** pupil names or identifiable details, SEN/safeguarding information, assessment data linked to individuals, anything confidential about colleagues or the school, or copyrighted resources you don't have rights to share. Describe the class in general terms instead ("a class with 4 EAL learners at an early stage").

### 0:20 to 0:40 | Hands-on in trios (20 min)
- **Mixed-subject trios** so people help each other and see AI used across subjects.
- **Task:** Each person plans part of their own real upcoming lesson. Use the recipe. Aim to get: a draft plan, plus one extra (a quiz, a differentiated task, a model answer, or a parent-friendly explanation).
- **Roles inside each trio (rotate at 10 minutes):** one person drives, one is the "challenger" who asks "is that actually right?" and "would that work with my class?", one notes what worked and what didn't.
- **Your job:** circulate, help with logins, unstick people. Prioritise the least confident and the sceptics, gently. Give quick prompt-help rather than doing it for them.
- **Fast finisher extension:** "Ask it to critique its own plan," or "Ask it for three alternative starters," or "Ask it to adapt this for a different year group."
- **For the very nervous:** offer a ready-made prompt on the handout they can paste and adapt, so they get a result in two minutes.

### 0:40 to 0:48 | Check it, and the sceptics' corner (8 min)
This section deals with accuracy and the doubts directly, and it's where you earn trust.

1. **Show your flawed output** (prepared earlier). Ask the room to find what's wrong. Reveal the error. Point: AI sounds confident when it is wrong.
2. **The checklist (on handout):**
   - Is it factually correct? (Check anything specific: dates, quotes, formulae, references, sources.)
   - Is it right for *this* class and the curriculum or exam spec?
   - Are the timings realistic?
   - Is there bias or stereotyping in the examples?
   - Would I be happy to put my name to this?
3. **Sceptics' corner:** invite concerns and answer honestly. Likely ones:
   - *"Isn't this cheating or deskilling?"* It's a drafting tool. Your professional judgement does the real work, and the time saved goes into the parts only you can do.
   - *"It's often wrong."* Yes, which is why we check. Treat it like a first draft from a trainee.
   - *"What about data protection?"* Hence the never-paste rules and the school policy.
   - *"What about pupils using it?"* A separate and important conversation. Flag it as a future session rather than trying to solve it now.
   - *"Environmental and ethical concerns."* Acknowledge them as real and unresolved. You don't have to have all the answers.
   - *"I don't have time to learn it."* The target is one lesson plan this week, ten minutes, not a new hobby.
   If you don't know an answer, say so and note it for follow-up. That's more credible than bluffing.

### 0:48 to 0:55 | Share back (7 min)
- Each trio gives one 30-second share: one thing that worked, one thing that didn't or surprised them.
- Capture on a whiteboard or shared document in two columns: **Worked well / Watch out for**. Share it afterwards as the staff's own guidance.
- Invite one or two volunteers (including a sceptic who has changed their mind a bit, if there is one) to show a good prompt or result.

### 0:55 to 1:00 | Commitments and close (5 min)
- Each person writes on a sticky note or the handout: **"This week I will try AI for ______."** Keep it small (a starter activity, a set of questions, a differentiated version).
- Remind of the one-sentence model: fast, keen, overconfident assistant; you are the teacher.
- Tell them what happens next (see below) and ask for a quick anonymous feedback poll (two questions: confidence out of 5 before and after; what would help next).
- Thank people, especially the sceptics, for their challenge.

## Handout (one side of A4)
1. The prompt recipe (R-C-L-C-F) with one worked example.
2. Five ready-to-paste prompts to adapt:
   - "Plan a [length] lesson on [topic] for Year [X]..."
   - "Write a starter activity that activates prior knowledge of [topic]."
   - "Create 8 retrieval questions with answers on [topic], from easy to hard."
   - "Rewrite this explanation for a pupil with a reading age of 9 / for EAL learners at an early stage."
   - "Give me three common misconceptions about [topic] and a quick activity to address each."
3. The "never paste" list.
4. The check-it checklist.
5. The school's approved tool(s) and where to find the policy.

## Differentiating for a mixed room
- **Least confident:** pre-written prompts, a trio partner who is more confident, and permission to just watch and ask questions for the first ten minutes.
- **Most confident:** extension challenges (critique, adapt, chain prompts), and invite them to be a floating helper.
- **Sceptics:** give them the "challenger" role, an explicit invitation to find where it fails, and no pressure to commit. Genuine critics improve the session and tend to be the most credible voices afterwards.
- **Subject variety:** in your demo, mention a quick second example from a very different subject (PE, languages, maths) so nobody thinks "this is not for my subject".

## Common pitfalls and how to avoid them
- **Logins eating the session.** Mitigate with the pre-session email and a login check in the first five minutes.
- **Wifi failing.** Have screenshots or a saved chat as backup.
- **Too much talk.** The hands-on block is the heart of the session. If you overrun, cut the demo's extra examples, not the hands-on.
- **Overselling.** Be honest about limits. Overclaiming loses the sceptics immediately.
- **Policy gaps.** If a question about data or policy can't be answered, say so and follow up.

## After the session
- Within 48 hours, send the handout, the "Worked well / Watch out for" list and the feedback poll.
- Offer a 20-minute drop-in a fortnight later ("bring a lesson, we'll plan it together").
- Set up a shared document or channel where staff post prompts that worked.
- Consider follow-up topics: using AI for differentiation and adaptation, and a separate session on pupils' use of AI and academic integrity.
- Review feedback and revisit after half a term: has anyone used it, and what did they learn?
