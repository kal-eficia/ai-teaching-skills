# Preparing for tomorrow's meeting

## Assumptions

- You're in a UK secondary school with a behaviour policy that sets out when detentions are given and how parents are told.
- The three detentions were given in line with that policy, and parents were informed each time. If they weren't, check this tonight, because it's the first thing a parent will ask.
- Your head of year (HoY) is attending as support and to chair, not to referee you.

## The mindset to walk in with

The mum has two separate concerns. Keep them apart in your head, because you'll answer them differently.

1. **"You're picking on her."** This is about fairness and consistency. You can answer it with facts.
2. **"She comes home crying and says you don't like her."** This is about how her daughter feels. You can't argue with a feeling, and you shouldn't try. You can take it seriously without agreeing that you're at fault.

Your goal is not to win. It is to leave with the parent feeling heard, the daughter feeling it's a fresh start, and a clear plan. If you "win" the argument and the mum leaves angry, the girl still has to sit in your lesson on Thursday.

Also hold this thought: both things can be true. The detentions can be fully justified and the girl can still feel picked on. Her distress is real information even if her interpretation ("she doesn't like me") is wrong. Wondering why a Year 9 girl feels this way is not an admission of guilt. It's good practice.

## Before the meeting (tonight)

**Gather the facts, so you can be specific and calm.**

- Dates of the three detentions, what each was for, and what you said or did beforehand (reminder, warning, seating change).
- Whether the school's behaviour system recorded them, and whether home was contacted each time.
- Her two other detentions: which teachers, for what, and whether the reasons are similar. Ask your HoY to bring this. It matters because it shows a pattern across staff, not a conflict with you. But be careful: don't use it to pile on. It should come from the HoY, framed gently, not from you.
- How many other students in the class got detentions for the same behaviours this half-term. If equipment and talking are routinely sanctioned for everyone, this is your best evidence of fairness. Know the number, but don't name other children.
- Any positives. Think of at least three genuine ones: a good piece of work, a helpful moment, a time she contributed. Write them down. If you struggle to come up with any, treat that as a signal to think about the relationship (see below).
- Her attainment and effort in your subject, if relevant.

**Have a quick word with your HoY beforehand (even five minutes).** Agree:
- Who opens and who leads.
- That the HoY will step in if it gets heated.
- What you're both prepared to offer (see "Possible outcomes").
- That the HoY will not undermine you in front of the parent, and you won't contradict them.

**Reflect honestly on three questions.** You don't need to share the answers, but you need to have thought about them:
- Is there any chance my tone or the way I've delivered the detentions has landed as harsh or public?
- Have I given her as many warm, low-key interactions as I give others?
- Is there anything about how I handle her talking (a public telling-off versus a quiet word) that I could change?

If the honest answer to any of these is "maybe", that's useful. Acknowledging it costs you very little and often defuses the whole conversation.

## Structure of the meeting

### 1. Open warmly (first two minutes)

Set the tone before anyone gets defensive. Something like:

> "Thank you for coming in, and for emailing me. I'm glad you did. I want [name] to feel good about being in my lessons, so I'm keen to hear your concerns and work out together how to make things better."

Avoid opening with the detention list. That makes it a trial.

### 2. Listen first

Invite her to speak, and let her finish. Don't interrupt, don't correct details yet, and take brief notes (this signals you're taking her seriously).

Useful prompts:
- "Can you tell me what [name] has been saying at home?"
- "What would you most like to be different?"

Reflect back what you heard before you respond: "So what I'm hearing is that she's coming home upset, and she feels I'm singling her out. Have I got that right?"

Most parents calm down noticeably once they've said it all and been repeated back accurately.

### 3. Acknowledge the feeling, not the accusation

You can say, truthfully:

> "I'm really sorry to hear she's been upset. I don't want any student to feel that way in my lessons, and it matters to me that she does."

> "I can see why, from what she's told you, it feels like that."

What you are not saying: "You're right, I've been unfair." What you are also not saying: "That's not true."

### 4. Give the facts, briefly and neutrally

Keep it short and factual, without adjectives. For example:

> "Let me explain where things stand so we're working from the same picture. This half-term she's had three detentions with me. Two were for [not having equipment] and one was for [talking over me / etc.]. [Whatever the accurate split is.] Each time I'd given a [reminder / warning] first, and the school was in touch with you."

Then:

> "The expectations are the same for the whole class, and other students have received the same sanction for the same things."

Say that last line only if it is true, and without naming anyone.

Two to three sentences per point is plenty. Don't read out a long list, and don't present it as a charge sheet.

If the HoY then mentions the two other detentions, let them frame it. Something like "We're seeing similar themes across a few lessons, which suggests this might be about organisation and settling in, not about one teacher."

### 5. Reframe towards the daughter's needs

This is where the meeting turns constructive. Gently open up the question of why:

- Equipment: "Is there anything getting in the way at home or in the morning that we could help with?" (Cost, a disorganised bag, shared custody between two homes, anxiety.) Offer practical help such as a pencil case kept in the classroom, a loan system without shaming, or a checklist.
- Talking: "I wonder whether she finds it hard to settle, or whether there's something going on socially in that class. Has she mentioned friendship issues?" Year 9 girls' friendship dynamics are a very common hidden cause of both upset and talking. A seating change may help, and may also tell you something.

Ask whether she has said what specifically feels unfair. That gives you concrete things to look at, rather than the general "she doesn't like me".

### 6. Agree a plan and a fresh start

Offer something specific that you will do. Even small, visible actions help the parent feel her concern landed:

- A quiet check-in with the daughter this week, led by you or the HoY, to hear things from her side.
- Giving her a reset: "From Thursday it's a clean slate."
- A private non-verbal reminder system for talking, so corrections aren't public.
- A seating review.
- A spare equipment solution so lack of equipment doesn't automatically become a sanction while you work on the root cause (check this fits your school policy first).
- Noticing and praising what she does well, and letting home know about it. A positive message home early is very powerful.
- A short review meeting or email in two to three weeks.

Ask what the parent would like to add, and ask what would help her daughter. Then, ask the parent to help in return: reinforce expectations at home, check the bag the night before.

### 7. Close well

Summarise the agreed actions out loud, and say you'll follow up in writing. End on something sincere:

> "Thank you again for raising this. I want her to do well in my lessons, and I think she can."

## Phrases that help

- "Help me understand more about..."
- "I can hear how worried you are."
- "We want the same thing here."
- "I'd like to look at this together."
- "I'm not going to pretend I get everything right, and I'm open to hearing if something I'm doing isn't working for her."

## Phrases to avoid

- "She's always..." or "She never..." (absolutes invite rebuttal)
- "That's not what happened." (Say instead: "That's not how I remember it, so I'd like to go through what I have.")
- "I treat everyone the same." On its own it sounds defensive. Use evidence instead.
- "She needs to learn..." (it sounds like blaming the child)
- Anything comparing her to named classmates.
- Jargon and policy references as a shield ("It's the policy").
- Anything that suggests the daughter is lying about how she feels. Her feelings are real, even if her explanation isn't accurate.

## If it gets difficult

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Mum is angry or raises her voice | Stay slow and quiet. Pause. "I can see how upset you are, and I want to sort this out. Can I tell you what I can do?" Let the HoY step in if it escalates. |
| Mum says "She never had problems before you" | "I'm glad to hear that. I'd like to understand what's different now so I can support her." Ask, don't defend. |
| Mum says "Other kids do the same and don't get detentions" | "I can see why it feels that way. I'm applying the same rules to everyone, and I'm happy to look at it again with [HoY]. Can you tell me what she's seen?" Then genuinely check it afterwards. If it turns out you've been inconsistent, own it. |
| Mum says "You clearly don't like her" | "I can hear that's how it feels. I do want her to do well, and I'd like to show her that. Here's what I'm going to do..." Don't argue the point. Demonstrate it. |
| Mum raises something new and serious (bullying, a specific incident, a safeguarding hint, SEN or mental health) | Stop and listen. Don't resolve it in the room. Say it will be taken seriously, hand it to the HoY, and follow the school's safeguarding or SEN procedures. Record it. |
| She asks for you to remove a detention | Don't agree or refuse on the spot. "I'll look at it with [HoY] and come back to you by [date]." |
| She wants to take it further or complain | Stay calm. "That's your right, and I'd rather we try to resolve it together first. Here's what I'm offering." Make sure the HoY notes it. |

## Possible outcomes

Be prepared for any of these, and be comfortable with each:

1. **Resolved with a plan.** Most common when the parent feels heard.
2. **Partial.** The parent leaves unconvinced but has agreed to a review. That's still fine.
3. **You discover something you didn't know.** The best result in many ways, even if uncomfortable. It could be a friendship issue, a learning need, a home circumstance, or something about your own approach.
4. **Complaint escalates.** Keep a record of everything you've done, and let the HoY lead.

## After the meeting

- Write short notes the same day: who was there, what was said, what was agreed, who's doing what, and the review date. Share them with your HoY.
- Send the parent a brief, friendly follow-up email (or check that the HoY will) confirming the agreed steps.
- Speak to the daughter in a low-key way at the start of your next lesson, e.g. "Good to see you." Don't mention the meeting, and don't go overboard. Aim to be warm and ordinary.
- Catch her doing something right within the first couple of lessons and acknowledge it quietly.
- Keep to the plan, and check in with your HoY in two to three weeks.

## One-page cheat sheet to take in

1. **Open warm.** Thank her for coming. State shared goal.
2. **Listen.** Don't interrupt. Reflect back.
3. **Acknowledge her feelings.** Not the accusation.
4. **Facts, briefly.** Three detentions, reasons, warnings, same rules for all.
5. **Ask why.** Equipment? Friendships? Settling? What's she said?
6. **Offer actions.** Fresh start, quieter reminders, seating, check-in, positive contact.
7. **Agree a review date.** Summarise out loud.
8. **Close kind.**

If you go in prepared with the facts, open to the possibility that there's something you can adjust, and focused on the daughter rather than on being proved right, this is very likely to go better than you fear. Parents who write emails like this are usually worried and want to be listened to. Good luck.
