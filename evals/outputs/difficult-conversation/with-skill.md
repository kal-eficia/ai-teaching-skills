# Meeting prep: parent concerned about "picking on" her daughter (Year 9)

**Assumptions (no teaching-context file used, so I've assumed the following):** the meeting is a normal pastoral meeting, roughly 20 to 30 minutes; your detentions were logged on your school's system with dates; the school behaviour policy allows detentions for missing equipment and talking over the teacher; no safeguarding concern has been raised. I've used "S" for the student. Keep her full name and any family details out of anything you write down or put into AI tools unless your school's data policy allows it.

---

## One-page brief

### 1. Facts (bring these on paper)
- Three detentions from you this half-term: for missing equipment and talking over you.
- Two further detentions from other teachers. This matters because it suggests a pattern across lessons, not something specific to you. Say it gently and only once (see "likely moments").
- **Before the meeting, pull the log** and write down for each detention: date, lesson, reason, what warnings came first, whether it was served, and how S responded. Also note how many warnings or equipment issues did *not* lead to a detention, and how many other students received the same sanction for the same behaviour. That last point is your best evidence of fairness.
- Note anything positive: effort, good work, moments of rapport. You will need it for the opening and for the plan.

**Facts vs interpretations**

| Fact | Interpretation (avoid presenting as fact) |
|---|---|
| Three detentions, dates and reasons as logged | "She's disruptive" / "She does it on purpose" |
| Mum reports S comes home crying | "You clearly don't like her" (Mum's belief, and a sincere one) |
| Two other teachers have also issued detentions | "It's a general attitude problem" |

### 2. Their view
Mum is hearing about school through a upset child. Underneath "picking on", she is probably worried that S is unhappy, feels singled out, and that nobody is on her side. She may be defensive, or feel that she's being judged as a parent. Taking this seriously is not the same as agreeing that you've done something wrong.

### 3. Your goal
**One outcome:** leave with Mum feeling heard, the facts understood, and a short, specific plan (with a review date) that makes S's lessons with you go better. A repaired relationship with S is the real aim, not winning the argument.

### 4. Negotiable and not negotiable
- **Not negotiable:** the behaviour expectations (equipment, listening when the teacher speaks) and that sanctions follow the school policy. You will not apologise for applying the policy consistently.
- **Negotiable:** how you support S to meet them. Ideas: a reminder before the lesson, a spare-equipment routine that does not embarrass her, a seat change, a quiet signal instead of a public warning, a brief positive check-in at the start of lessons, a review in two to three weeks.

### 5. Who attends
Your head of year will be there. **Before the meeting, agree with them (10 minutes):** who chairs, who speaks first, that you'll share the facts and they'll hold the wider picture (the other two detentions, any pastoral concerns), and that they will step in if it gets heated. Check whether the school's complaints policy treats this email as a formal complaint. If it does, follow that procedure. If anything suggests S is distressed beyond school (or that her crying points to something else), pass it to your designated safeguarding lead rather than handling it in the meeting.

## The conversation

**Opening (about 30 seconds)**
> "Thank you for coming in. I want to start by saying I'm glad you told me how S has been feeling, because we both want her to do well and to feel good about coming to school. I'd like to hear what she's said to you first, then go through what I've seen, and then agree a plan together."

**Listen first.** Let her finish. Take brief notes. Then summarise:
> "So what I'm hearing is that S comes home upset, she feels I'm singling her out, and you're worried that she's unhappy in my lessons. Have I got that right?"

**Acknowledge the feeling, not the claim.**
> "It's really hard to hear that your daughter has been in tears, and I'm sorry she's been upset. I don't want any student to feel picked on, and I'd like to work out what's going on."

(That is true and kind, and it does not agree that you have picked on her.)

**Facts, not labels.** Share the log calmly:
> "This half-term S has had three detentions in my lessons: on [date] and [date] for not having her equipment, and on [date] for talking while I was teaching. Each time she'd had a reminder first. The same rule applies to everyone in the class."

Then, only if relevant and with your head of year's agreement:
> "[Head of year] can also say a bit about the wider picture, because there have been a couple of similar concerns elsewhere, which makes me think we should tackle this together."

**Turn to the positives and the plan.**
> "There's a lot I like about S: [specific strength]. I'd like to build on that."

**Agree next steps** (write them down in the meeting):
1. **You:** a private reminder or quiet signal before a warning; a spare-equipment routine; a quick positive check-in; a seat change if useful.
2. **S:** bring her equipment (checklist in her planner or bag the night before); aim for a clean slate in your lessons for the next two weeks.
3. **Mum:** if S comes home upset, a short email to you or the head of year the same day, so you can find out what happened while it is fresh. Also ask her to help with the equipment routine at home.
4. **Review:** you and the head of year check in with Mum (email or call) in about two to three weeks.

**Close on the shared goal.**
> "Thank you again. We all want S to enjoy school and do well, and I think this plan gives her a good chance."

## Likely moments, and calm responses

1. **Anger: "You're picking on her! Other kids talk and nothing happens."**
   > "I can hear how frustrated you are. I'd feel the same if my child was upset. I apply the same rule to every student, and I can show you how the sanctions are recorded. If S has seen others get away with things, I'd like to know, because it matters to me that it's fair."
   (Stay slow and quiet. Don't match their volume. Let your head of year step in if it escalates.)

2. **Blame: "You clearly don't like her."**
   > "I'm sorry it feels like that, and I do want S to know that isn't true. I like having her in my class, and I want her to succeed. The detentions were about specific behaviours, not about her as a person, and I'd like to show her that."

3. **Tears (Mum's or about S).**
   Pause. Offer tissues and a moment. Then:
   > "Take your time. I'd like to understand what S has said, so I can put it right if there's something I've missed."

4. **"The other teachers don't have a problem with her" or "It's only you."**
   Don't argue or list others' sanctions as a charge. Hand to your head of year:
   > "I'll let [head of year] speak to that, but my focus today is on what we can do in my lessons."
   (If she says it's only you, the other two detentions are factual, but present them as "we're all seeing something similar, so let's support her", not "see, it's not just me".)

5. **A request you can't grant: "Take the detentions off her record" / "Don't give her any more detentions."**
   > "I can't promise that, because the same rules have to apply to everyone. What I can do is make sure she gets a reminder first, and that we start afresh from today. If she meets the expectations, there won't be anything to record."
   (If she wants the existing ones reviewed, that is a decision for your head of year or the policy, not for you on the spot.)

6. **"She says you shout / humiliate her in front of the class."**
   This is the one to take seriously. Don't defend or dismiss. Listen, then:
   > "Thank you for telling me. I'd want to hear exactly what S experienced. I don't intend to embarrass anyone, so I'll make sure I give any reminders privately."
   Note it and tell your head of year afterwards. It is worth reflecting honestly on whether your tone in those moments could have felt public or sharp. If she describes anything beyond that, follow the school's procedure.

---

## Do and don't
- **Do:** bring the log, a pen, and the plan on paper; sit alongside rather than opposite; speak last on facts; keep it to the next steps.
- **Don't:** speculate about S's character, compare her with named classmates, discuss other students, argue over who is right, or promise things the policy doesn't allow.
- **Take a minute before you go in** and decide: what is the one thing I most want Mum to leave knowing? (Suggested: "Mr/Ms X wants S to succeed, and we have a plan.")

As we agreed:
- I will give S a private reminder before any formal warning, set up a spare-equipment routine, and check in with her briefly at the start of lessons.
- S will bring her equipment each lesson, and we will start afresh from today.
- If S comes home upset, please email me or [head of year] that day so we can look into it straight away.
- [Head of year] and I will be in touch again on [date, in 2 to 3 weeks] to see how things are going.

I want S to enjoy my lessons and do well in them, and I think this plan gives her a good start.

Kind regards,
[Your name]
