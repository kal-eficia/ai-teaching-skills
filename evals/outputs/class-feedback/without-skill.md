# Whole-class feedback: Priestley's presentation of Mr Birling

Your notes point to a clear plan: give the class one shared feedback session instead of 28 sets of comments. Below is a ready-to-use package that takes roughly 5 minutes to prepare and about 30 to 40 minutes of lesson time.

**Assumptions:** I've assumed a GCSE-style essay (AQA or similar) and a normal lesson of around an hour. The "model" sentences are written for you to adapt or replace with the best examples from your own pile.

---

## 1. The headline message (say this first, 2 minutes)

> "Your plot knowledge is secure. That is the hard part done. Now the marks come from **what Priestley is doing and why**, not from what happens. Today we're moving from *telling me the story* to *explaining the writer's choices*."

Start with the positives: strong plot knowledge across the class, and a handful of essays that linked Birling to capitalism in a genuinely perceptive way. Name no pupils, or ask permission first, then read out one or two of those capitalism links anonymously as a model.

---

## 2. Three priorities for improvement (not six)

Giving a class too many targets means they act on none. Your notes show six issues. I'd rank them like this:

| Priority | Issue | Why it matters most |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Retelling instead of analysing | Biggest mark-limiter across the whole class |
| **2** | Quotes without comment on language | Half the class; easy to fix with a formula |
| **3** | Missing context (1912 vs 1945) | Almost nobody did it, and it is the quickest "win" |
| Quick fixes | Long paragraphs; spelling | Handle with a checklist (section 5), not teaching time |

The capitalism link is your strength to build on: it is already context in disguise, so you can use it as the bridge to priority 3.

---

## 3. Mini-lesson content for each priority

### Priority 1: Stop retelling. Ask "so what?"

**Show on the board (anonymised/invented example):**

- *Retelling:* "Mr Birling says he doesn't care about Eva Smith and sacks her for asking for more money. Then the Inspector arrives and asks him about it."
- *Analysing:* "Priestley presents Birling as selfish and short-sighted by having him dismiss Eva's strike as a threat to profit, so that the audience are encouraged to judge his values harshly."

**The tool: "So what?" test.** After every sentence, ask "so what does this show about Birling, and why did Priestley want us to see it?" If the sentence can't answer that, it is probably retelling. Pupils annotate a paragraph of their own essay: underline retelling in one colour, analysis in another.

**Sentence stems:**
- "Priestley presents Birling as ... to show ..."
- "This suggests that ..."
- "The audience would ..."

### Priority 2: Quote, then zoom in on a word

**The formula (Point, Evidence, Zoom):**
1. **Point:** what you are arguing.
2. **Evidence:** a short quote (3 to 8 words is plenty).
3. **Zoom:** pick *one word or technique* and say what it suggests.

**Model:** Birling calls the Titanic "unsinkable" and says "absolutely unsinkable". Priestley's repetition of the absolute adverb "absolutely" makes Birling sound arrogantly certain, so the audience, who know the ship sank, see how wrong he is.

Pick a quote the class already knows and run a 3-minute whole-class "zoom" on it: *"a hard-headed, practical man of business"*, *"the Titanic ... unsinkable"*, *"a man has to mind his own business and look after himself"*.

**Key language terms to push:** dramatic irony, repetition, adjectives, verbs, hyperbole, tone, stage directions stating his manner ("rather portentous").

### Priority 3: Add the 1912 / 1945 lens

Most of the class left this out, so teach it as one short, memorable idea:

> **Set in 1912, written in 1945.** Priestley knows what Birling doesn't: two world wars, the Titanic sinking, the Great Depression. Birling's confident predictions ("there'll be no war") are wrong, and the 1945 audience knows it.

**Context sentence to add to any paragraph:**
> "Writing in 1945, after two world wars, Priestley uses Birling's ... to show an audience who had lived through ... that ..."

**Link to capitalism (building on your strong essays):** Birling embodies the capitalist, "look after yourself" view. Priestley, a socialist, wrote the play in 1945 as the country was building the welfare state, so he uses Birling as the voice of an outdated, selfish ideology the audience should reject.

**Rule of thumb:** one context sentence per paragraph, and it must connect to the point, not sit on its own as a fact dump.

---

## 4. A quick activity to make it stick (10 to 15 minutes)

**"Upgrade a paragraph."** Give pupils a deliberately weak anonymised paragraph (write one yourself or lightly adapt a real one). In pairs they:
1. Cross out the retelling.
2. Add a "zoom" on one word.
3. Add one context sentence.
4. Cut it to 5 to 7 lines, split into two paragraphs if needed.

Then they **redraft one paragraph of their own essay** in the same lesson. This is your assessment of whether the feedback landed, and you only need to scan the redrafts, not mark them in depth.

---

## 5. Class checklist for paragraphs and spelling (hand out or project)

**Paragraph check**
- [ ] One main idea per paragraph (if you have "also" or "another thing", start a new one)
- [ ] About 6 to 10 lines maximum
- [ ] Does it contain: point, short quote, zoom on a word, context link?

**Spelling: three words everyone writes on the board**
- **Priestley** (not Priestly or Priestly): *Priest-ley*, there is a "t" before the "ley"
- **capitalism**: *capital-ism*, the word "capital" plus "ism"
- **responsibility**: *respons-i-bil-ity*, think "response" + "ability": the ability to respond

Suggested routine: pupils write each word three times in the front of their book and add them to a personal spelling list. A 3-word "mini-test" at the start of the next lesson takes two minutes.

---

## 6. Low-effort personal feedback (so every pupil still gets something)

You said you can't write on every essay. You don't have to:

1. **Number your three targets** (T1 = analyse not retell, T2 = zoom on language, T3 = context) and **write one number or two on the front of each essay**. That is about 10 seconds per script.
2. **Tick or highlight one strong sentence** per essay so each pupil sees a specific positive.
3. Pupils then **copy the matching target and stems into their book** and respond in the redraft (section 4).
4. Optional: a quick WWW/EBI line only for the borderline or very weak scripts.

---

## 7. Follow-up

- **Next lesson:** a 5-minute retrieval starter (the three spellings plus "What is the point, evidence, zoom formula?").
- **In two weeks:** a short timed paragraph on Birling, or on Sheila or Mr Birling versus the Inspector, to check the targets have transferred.
- **Tracking:** jot down which pupils still retell, so you can group them for a small-group catch-up later.

---

## Summary: what to give back to the class

1. A positive headline (strong plot knowledge, and the capitalism links).
2. **Three** priorities, not six: analyse (not retell), zoom on language, add context.
3. A model paragraph that shows retelling versus analysis.
4. A short in-lesson redraft task.
5. A one-page checklist covering paragraphs and the three spellings.
6. A quick number-code on each essay so everyone gets personal direction without you writing comments.
