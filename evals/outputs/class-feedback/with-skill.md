# Year 10 whole-class feedback: How does Priestley present Mr Birling in *An Inspector Calls*?

You don't need to write on all 28 essays. Give the class the one-page sheet below, write a single code letter (A to D) on each essay, and run the 15-minute improvement task next lesson. The sheet is student-facing. The teacher notes at the end are for you only.

**Assumptions I've made:** the essays were on the whole play, they were marked against the usual GCSE English Literature objectives (analysis of language, then context), and the class has access to the play text. I've written the quotations from memory, so check them against your edition before printing. The wording of the student examples is mine. I haven't seen any essays, so swap in anonymous lines from real ones if you have time.

## Student sheet: Your Birling essay feedback

### 1. What went well

- **You know the play.** Almost all of you showed secure knowledge of the plot and of what Birling says and does. That's a solid base to build on.
- **Some of you made a big, clever point about capitalism.** The strongest essays linked Birling to capitalism, showing that he stands for a whole attitude to money and society and isn't only a character. This is the kind of thinking that earns top marks. Keep it.
- **Many of you used quotations.** Choosing a quotation to back up a point is the right habit. Now we make the quotations work harder.

### 2. What to fix: find your code

Your teacher has written a letter on your essay. Find it below and do the matching fix in the task.

**A. Story, not analysis.** You tell me what happens. I want you to tell me what Priestley is doing.

- Retelling: *"Birling says the Titanic is unsinkable and then the Inspector arrives."*
- Analysing: *"Priestley has Birling call the Titanic 'unsinkable' to make the audience distrust everything else he says."*
- Test for every sentence: is it about what happens, or about why Priestley wrote it that way? Aim for the second. Start sentences with **Priestley presents... / Priestley uses... / This makes the audience...**

**B. Quotation with no comment on the language.** You gave me the quotation, then moved on.

- Pick out one word or phrase from it and say what it suggests. Ask: what does this word make us think about Birling, and what does Priestley want the audience to feel?
- Example quotation: *"hard-headed, practical man of business"*. Which word shows he's proud? What does "hard-headed" suggest about how he treats people?
- Pattern to copy: **The word "___" suggests ___, which makes the audience feel ___.**

**C. Missing context (1912 vs 1945).** The play is set in 1912, but Priestley wrote it in 1945, just after the Second World War. That gap is the point.

- In 1912, Birling's confidence ("unsinkable", "there isn't a chance of war") sounds sensible. The 1945 audience knows that he's wrong, because they've lived through two world wars.
- Priestley was a socialist, so he uses Birling to show why the old attitude of "look after yourself" had to change.
- Pattern to copy: **Priestley's 1945 audience would know that ___, so Birling's words ___.**

**D. Paragraphs too long.** Your good ideas are getting lost.

- Aim for **one idea per paragraph**: point, quotation, language comment, context link, and a closing sentence.
- If a paragraph runs past about 10 lines, find where the second idea starts and begin a new paragraph there.

### 3. Model paragraph

This is one paragraph that does all four things well. I've labelled where the marks come from.

> Priestley presents Mr Birling as a selfish capitalist who puts profit above people. **(Point: answers the question)** In Act One, Birling announces that "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own". **(Short, well-chosen quotation)** The repetition of "his own" makes Birling sound narrow and self-centred, suggesting that he sees society as a collection of separate people who owe each other nothing. **(Comment on specific words, not just "this shows he is selfish")** Priestley wrote the play in 1945, when Britain was building the welfare state after two world wars, so his audience would see Birling's attitude as outdated and dangerous. **(Context linked to the point)** He is a warning of what happens when a country ignores its responsibilities to others. **(Links back to the question)**

Notice what it doesn't do: it doesn't retell what happens next in the scene.

### 4. Spelling and key words

Check these in your essay and fix any you got wrong. Copy each one out correctly three times.

| Word | Tip |
|---|---|
| **Priestley** | Priest + ley. It's the author's name, so you must get it right every time. |
| **capitalism** | capital + ism (it has an "ital" in the middle). |
| **responsibility** | respons**ibility**: the ending is "ibility", not "ability". It's an important word in this play. |

### 5. Your task (10 to 15 minutes)

Look up your code and do **one** of these. Write it in green pen or underneath your essay.

- **A:** Pick one paragraph that retells. Rewrite it in 4 or 5 sentences so every sentence says *what Priestley is doing and why*.
- **B:** Choose two quotations from your essay. Under each, write 2 sentences commenting on one word or phrase, using the pattern above.
- **C:** Write 3 sentences connecting one Birling quotation to what the 1945 audience knew. Use the pattern above.
- **D:** Take your longest paragraph and rewrite it as two shorter paragraphs. Each must have its own point and quotation.

**Everyone:** correct your three spellings, and write one sentence on how Birling links to capitalism if you haven't already.

## Teacher notes (not for students)

**How to use the codes.** On each essay, write only the single most useful letter. Most students will need A, B or C. If you're short of time, give B or C to anyone who used quotations but didn't comment on them. About half the class falls into that group. D can go on top of any other code if you have a second to add it. Anyone whose essay already analyses well and uses context can get no code, plus the "extend" task below.

**Extend task for stronger students (those who linked Birling to capitalism):** write a paragraph comparing how Birling and the Inspector present responsibility, using one quotation from each.

**Reteach to the whole class (priority order):**

1. **Retelling vs analysing.** This is the biggest barrier. Spend 5 minutes live: put one retelling sentence on the board and rewrite it together as an analysing sentence.
2. **Language analysis of a short quotation.** Model it with a single quotation and zoom in on one word. Tell them a short quotation with detailed comment beats a long one with none.
3. **Context.** Teach the 1912 / 1945 gap explicitly (the Titanic and the First World War in 1912 against the Second World War and the 1945 election), and show them how to attach it to a quotation instead of leaving it in an introduction.
4. **Paragraph structure.** Give them the point, quotation, language, context, link frame. It also helps with item 1.
5. **Spelling.** Add the three words to a starter quiz for a week.

**Why this works:** it takes you about 5 minutes to code 28 essays, instead of hours of writing, and every student has a specific, targeted task. If you'd like to check progress, collect the rewritten paragraphs next lesson and glance through them, or ask students to swap with a partner.
