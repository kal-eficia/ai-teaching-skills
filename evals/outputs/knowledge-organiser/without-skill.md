# Year 7 Knowledge Organiser: The Norman Conquest, 1066

**Big question: How did William of Normandy win the English crown in 1066, and how did he keep it?**

*Assumptions: this is a one-page-style organiser for students to learn from, aimed at a mixed-ability Year 7 class, followed by a short self-quiz. Dates and facts follow the standard KS3 account. Print it double-sided and ask students to cover the right-hand column to test themselves.*

## 2. The four claimants

| Claimant | Who he was | His claim | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Harold Godwinson** | Earl of Wessex, the most powerful noble in England, Edward's brother-in-law | Said Edward named him heir on his deathbed. Chosen by the Witan (the king's council of nobles). | On the spot, English, rich, experienced soldier | Not royal blood. Had sworn an oath to William. |
| **William, Duke of Normandy** | Powerful ruler in northern France | Said Edward had promised him the throne years before, and that Harold swore to support him | Strong army, skilled cavalry, the Pope's blessing | Had to cross the Channel. Not English. |
| **Harald Hardrada** | King of Norway, famous Viking warrior | Based on an old agreement between earlier Norwegian and English kings. Helped by Harold's exiled brother Tostig. | Fierce, experienced army and a large fleet | Weak claim. Far from home. |
| **Edgar Ætheling** | Teenage great-nephew of Edward | Closest royal blood | Real family link to the throne | Too young, little support or power |

**Key words:** *claimant* (someone who says the throne is rightfully theirs), *Witan* (council of the most important nobles), *oath* (a serious promise, made before God).

## 3. The three battles of 1066

### Stamford Bridge (25 September 1066), near York
- Harold had been waiting on the south coast for William. Hardrada and Tostig landed in the north instead.
- Harold marched his army north very fast (roughly 180 miles in about four days) and took the Vikings by surprise.
- The English won a huge victory. **Hardrada and Tostig were both killed.**
- Only a small part of the Viking fleet sailed home.
- **Why it matters:** Harold removed one claimant, but his army was tired and had lost men.

### Hastings (14 October 1066), near Hastings, Sussex
- Just days after Stamford Bridge, Harold heard that William had landed. He rushed south to meet him.
- Harold's army: mostly **infantry**, fighting on foot in a **shield wall** on top of a hill (Senlac Hill).
- William's army: **archers, cavalry (knights on horseback) and infantry**.
- The English shield wall held for hours. Then some English soldiers chased what looked like a Norman retreat, and the wall broke up. Many historians think the Normans used a **feigned (pretend) retreat**.
- **Harold was killed.** Tradition says by an arrow in the eye, but the evidence is unclear.
- William built **Battle Abbey** on the site afterwards.

### Why did William win at Hastings? (learn at least four)
1. Harold's army was **exhausted** after marching north and back south.
2. William had **cavalry and archers**, the English had no cavalry and few archers.
3. The English **broke the shield wall** by chasing the Normans downhill.
4. William was a **strong, organised leader** and had planned the invasion carefully.
5. **Luck**: the wind kept William in port until after Harold had marched north, and Harold was killed.
6. Harold had **less time to gather his full army**, as many men had gone home after the harvest.

## 4. How William kept control

### Castles
- William built castles in every important town. Early ones were **motte and bailey**: built quickly out of wood.
  - **Motte** = a large mound of earth, with a wooden tower on top (the keep).
  - **Bailey** = a walled yard below, for animals, soldiers and workshops.
- Later castles were rebuilt in **stone**, such as the **Tower of London** (the White Tower) and **Windsor**.
- **Purpose:** a safe base for soldiers, a way to control the land around, and a clear sign of Norman power.
- By 1100 there were around **500 castles**.

### The feudal system
A pyramid of loyalty. Each person held land from the person above in return for service.

| Level | Role | What they gave |
|---|---|---|
| **King (William)** | Owned all the land in England | Land and protection |
| **Barons and bishops** (tenants-in-chief) | Held huge areas from the king | Money, **knights** and loyalty |
| **Knights** | Held smaller areas from the barons | Fighting service, usually about 40 days a year |
| **Peasants** (villeins) | Farmed the land and were not free to leave | Work, food and rent to their lord |

- Most **Anglo-Saxon nobles lost their land**, which was given to Normans William trusted.
- In **1086** at Salisbury, landholders swore an **oath of loyalty directly to William**, not just to their own lord.

**Key words:** *feudal system*, *baron*, *knight*, *peasant*, *tenant-in-chief*, *loyalty*.

### The Domesday Book (1086)
- **What:** a huge written survey of who owned what across England.
- **Why:** to find out how much land and wealth England had, so William could **collect taxes** and be certain who owed him loyalty and service. Also useful as a record in case of invasion (a Danish threat was feared in 1085).
- **How:** royal officials (commissioners) visited each area and questioned local people under oath. They asked about land, buildings, ploughs, animals, mills and people.
- **Covers:** about **13,000 places** in England.
- **Why "Domesday"?** The English said it was as final as **Doomsday (the Day of Judgement)**: there was no appeal against what was written.
- **Why it matters:** it showed the king's power and gave historians an amazing picture of life in England. The book can still be seen today.

### Harsh methods
- **Harrying of the North (1069 to 1070):** after northern rebellions, William's army destroyed villages, crops and animals. Tens of thousands may have died of hunger. This shows William could be ruthless to keep control.
- **Hereward the Wake** led English resistance from the Isle of Ely until about 1071.

## 5. Key evidence

- **The Bayeux Tapestry:** an embroidered cloth about 70 metres long, made soon after 1066. It tells the Norman story of events. It was probably made for the Normans, so ask: *whose side is it telling?*
- **The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle:** a year-by-year record kept by English monks.
- **The Domesday Book:** official record of England in 1086.

## 7. Self-quiz (cover the page and answer from memory)

1. Which king died on 5 January 1066, and why was this a problem?
2. Name the four claimants.
3. Who won the Battle of Stamford Bridge, and who was killed?
4. Where did William land, and on what date?
5. Give **three** reasons William won at Hastings.
6. What is a motte and bailey castle? Draw one and label it.
7. Describe the feudal system in order, from the top.
8. Why was the Domesday Book made, and why was it given that name?
9. Give **two** ways William kept control of England.
10. Which do you think was most important in keeping control: castles, the feudal system or the Domesday Book? Explain with evidence.

### Answers
1. Edward the Confessor. He had no children, so several people claimed the throne.
2. Harold Godwinson, William of Normandy, Harald Hardrada, Edgar Ætheling.
3. Harold's English army won. Hardrada and Tostig were killed.
4. Pevensey, 28 September 1066.
5. Any three from section 3 (tired English army, Norman cavalry and archers, broken shield wall, strong leadership, luck).
6. A wooden tower on a mound (motte) with a walled yard (bailey) below. Labels: keep, motte, bailey, palisade, ditch.
7. King, then barons and bishops, then knights, then peasants.
8. To find out who owned what so William could tax and control the country. Named after Doomsday because its decisions could not be appealed.
9. Any two from castles, the feudal system, replacing English nobles with Normans, the Domesday Book, force (Harrying of the North).
10. Open answer: must pick one, give evidence and explain why it mattered more than the others.

## 8. Think like a historian

- **Cause:** What made 1066 a year of crisis? (No heir, several claimants.)
- **Consequence:** What changed for ordinary English people after 1066? (New lords, new language of the ruling class, castles, taxes.)
- **Significance:** Was 1066 a complete turning point or did some things stay the same? (The Normans kept many Anglo-Saxon systems, such as shires and sheriffs.)
- **Sources:** Who made the source, and why? Does it show only one side?
