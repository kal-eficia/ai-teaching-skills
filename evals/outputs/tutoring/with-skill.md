## 1. Retrieval starter (8 min)

**Say to her:** "No notes, no searching, no AI. For each one, write Guessing, Think so or Certain next to your answer."

### Student sheet

1. An atom of chlorine has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. How many electrons does it have, and what is its mass number?
2. Write the formula of magnesium chloride.
3. Why do elements in the same group of the periodic table react in a similar way?
4. Sodium reacts with chlorine to make sodium chloride. Describe what happens to the electrons.
5. Describe the arrangement and movement of the particles in a liquid.
6. Write the formula of sodium oxide.
7. Chlorine has the electronic structure 2,8,7. How many more electrons does it need to have a full outer shell?

### Tutor key

| Q | Answer | Topic | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 17 electrons; mass number 35 | Atomic structure | Older topic, due a check. Needed for every electronic structure question. |
| 2 | MgCl2 | Ionic formulae | She said she was guessing last week. Confidence rating tells you whether she's still guessing. |
| 3 | Same number of outer-shell electrons | Periodic table | Older topic. It links directly to why Group 1 ions are 1+ and Group 2 are 2+, which today's method uses. |
| 4 | Sodium atom loses one electron, becoming Na+. Chlorine atom gains it, becoming Cl-. | Ionic bonding | Last lesson's thread. She drew this correctly, so check she can say it. |
| 5 | Close together, irregular arrangement, move around and past each other | States of matter | Older topic, and a prerequisite for the melting point explanation: she needs to know what "melting" means for particles. |
| 6 | Na2O | Ionic formulae | Second formula, with the 2 on the metal. Today's paired example is the same pattern. |
| 7 | 1 | Electronic structure | Prerequisite for covalent bonding next week. Retrieval of something already taught, not a pre-test. |

Topics alternate, so no two neighbours come from the same topic.

**What to watch:** if Q2 or Q6 is "Certain" and wrong, that's the most useful signal of the session. Note it, say nothing yet, and return to it in Teach B. If Q5 is weak, spend an extra minute on it at the start of Teach A.

## 2. Homework review (5 min)

Don't re-mark in silence. Ask:

1. "Pick one of the six you're sure about. Talk me through how you got the formula."
2. "Pick one you weren't sure about. What did you do when you got stuck?"

Listen for: remembering formulae as fixed strings, copying the number of the other ion's charge without knowing why, or putting a subscript on the wrong element. Don't correct yet. Say: "Good, that's useful. I'll show you a method in a few minutes that means you never need to guess."

## 3. Teach (15 min)

### Teach A: Why ionic compounds have high melting points (8 min)

**Opening (1 min).** "Last week your diagram of NaCl was perfect. But look at it. It shows one sodium and one chlorine. A melting point is about billions of ions together. So today we draw what the diagram leaves out."

**Draw or share a simple 2D lattice** (alternating + and - in a grid). Point out: every ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions, in all directions, and the pattern repeats. Call it a **giant ionic lattice**.

**Build the answer as a three-link chain** (say each link aloud, she repeats it):

1. **Structure:** a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions.
2. **Force:** strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions, acting in all directions.
3. **Energy:** a lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces, so the melting point is high.

**Model answer (read it, then she reads it back):**
> Sodium chloride has a giant ionic lattice. There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions, in all directions. A lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces, so it has a high melting point.

**Thinking aloud:** "Melting means the ions can move past each other. For that, the attractions holding them in place have to be overcome. Lots of strong attractions, lots of energy."

**Common slips to name now (30 seconds):**
- Saying "the atoms" or "the molecules" instead of **ions**. Ionic compounds don't have molecules.
- Saying "strong ionic bonds" and stopping there. The mark is for the **forces between oppositely charged ions**, and for the **large amount of energy**.
- Saying the compound "breaks down" or "the bond breaks" as if one pair of atoms is separated.

**Explain-it-back (1 min):** "Tell me the three links without looking."

**Plant a seed for next week:** "Next week you'll meet substances with low melting points. Hold on to this chain, because you'll compare them."

### Teach B: A formulae method with no guessing (7 min)

Don't use criss-cross. Teach charge balancing.

**The four steps, the same every time:**

1. Write each ion with its charge (from the group number, or the given charge).
2. Make the total positive charge equal the total negative charge.
3. Write the formula, using the numbers that balance it. Leave out a 1. Use brackets for compound ions.
4. Check: add up the charges. Do they cancel to zero?

**Group-to-charge shortcut (show on the board):**

| Group | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ion charge | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 3- | 2- | 1- |

**Fully worked example: magnesium chloride** (the one she was guessing):

| Step | Working | Thinking |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mg2+ and Cl- | Magnesium is Group 2, so it loses 2 electrons. Chlorine is Group 7, so it gains 1. |
| 2 | One Mg2+ gives +2. I need -2 in total. One Cl- gives -1, so I need two. | The positives and negatives must cancel. |
| 3 | MgCl2 | The 2 goes next to the ion there are two of. |
| 4 | +2 + (2 x -1) = 0 | The check is what tells you you're right. |

**Paired example: sodium oxide.** It differs in one way: the 2 now goes on the metal.

| Step | Working | Thinking |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Na+ and O2- | Group 1 loses 1. Group 6 gains 2. |
| 2 | One O2- gives -2. I need +2, so two Na+. | Same method, but now the metal needs the 2. |
| 3 | Na2O | |
| 4 | (2 x +1) + (-2) = 0 | |

**Point out:** the method is the same, the answer changes. That's why guessing a pattern fails and the method doesn't.

**Where students most often go wrong (highlight it):** changing the little number *inside* the symbol, or writing MgCl when the charges don't cancel. Step 4 catches both.

**Warn about the Mg2O2 trap.** Magnesium oxide is Mg2+ and O2-: one of each, so MgO. Write the simplest ratio.

## 4. Guided practice (10 min)

You prompt, she does. Faded support: she does more of the steps each time. Use the same four steps and the same table layout.

### Part 1: Formulae (6 min)

**Faded 1: lithium oxide.** Steps 1 to 3 are shown, she does step 4.
- Step 1: Li+ and O2-
- Step 2: Need +2 to cancel the O2-, so two Li+.
- Step 3: Li2O
- Step 4 (she does): (2 x +1) + (-2) = 0. Correct.

**Faded 2: aluminium chloride.** Steps 1 and 2 are given, she does steps 3 and 4.
- Step 1: Al3+ and Cl-
- Step 2: Al3+ gives +3. I need -3, so three Cl-.
- She writes: **AlCl3**. Check: +3 + (3 x -1) = 0.

**Faded 3: magnesium oxide.** Only step 1 is given: Mg2+ and O2-.
- She does 2 to 4. **MgO**. Check: +2 + -2 = 0.
- Prompt only if stuck: "How many of each do you need?" If she writes Mg2O2, ask "Is there a simpler ratio?"

### Part 2: Melting point with a frame (4 min)

**Question:** Explain why calcium oxide has a high melting point. (3 marks)

**Give her the frame** (blanks are hers to fill):
> Calcium oxide has a giant ________ lattice. There are strong ________ forces of attraction between ________ charged ions. A ________ amount of ________ is needed to overcome these forces.

**Answer:** ionic; electrostatic; oppositely; large; energy.

**Tutor prompts:** "Which link in the chain is this?" "What are the particles here: atoms, molecules or ions?"

## 5. Independent practice (15 min)

Watch her method, not just her answers. Say little. Note which step she skips.

### Part 1: Formulae (7 min)

Write the formula of each. She must show the four steps, at least for I3 and I4.

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| I1 | sodium fluoride | NaF |
| I2 | calcium bromide | CaBr2 |
| I3 | aluminium sulfide | Al2S3 (Al3+ x 2 = +6; S2- x 3 = -6) |
| I4 | magnesium nitrate (nitrate ion is NO3-) | Mg(NO3)2. Brackets needed. The twist: a compound ion. |
| I5 | A student writes magnesium chloride as MgCl. Explain the mistake and correct it. | The charges don't cancel: Mg2+ is +2, Cl- is -1, total +1. Needs two Cl-: MgCl2. |

### Part 2: Exam-style (6 min)

**Question:** Explain why magnesium oxide has a high melting point. (4 marks)

**Mark scheme (1 mark each, up to 4):**
1. Giant ionic lattice (structure).
2. Oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic forces of attraction.
3. Strong forces / acting in all directions (through the lattice).
4. A lot of / large amount of energy needed to overcome (break) these forces.

**Do not credit:** "strong ionic bonds" alone, "atoms" or "molecules", "the bond between Mg and O breaks" with no mention of forces between ions or energy.

**Stretch (only if she finishes early):** "Magnesium oxide melts at about 2850 C and sodium chloride at about 800 C. Suggest why." Answer: Mg2+ and O2- have bigger charges, so the electrostatic attraction is stronger and more energy is needed.

### Part 3: Discuss (2 min)

Go through the answers. Ask: "Which step did you find hardest?" "Did the check catch anything?"

## 6. Exit check (5 min)

Closed-book, no help. Ask for a confidence rating (Guessing / Think so / Certain) on each.

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | Write the formula of aluminium fluoride. | AlF3 |
| E2 | Write the formula of potassium sulfide. | K2S |
| E3 | Give three points that explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point. (3 marks) | Giant ionic lattice; strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions; lots of energy to overcome them. |

**How to read it:** E1 and E2 test whether the method has stuck on unfamiliar compounds. E3 tests whether she can produce the chain unprompted. If she gets E3 with prompts only, repeat the chain at the start of next session. If Certain and wrong on E1 or E2, that's the first item in next week's retrieval.

## 7. Set homework (2 min)

About 20 minutes, doable alone. Include the answers when you send it.

1. Explain why potassium chloride has a high melting point. (4 marks)
2. Write the formula of each: (a) calcium fluoride, (b) potassium oxide, (c) aluminium nitride (stretch; both ions are 3 so the ratio is simple).
3. Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for magnesium chloride. Show the charges. (This reviews last week's topic and links to the formula.)
4. Get ready for covalent bonding. Copy and complete, using the periodic table:

| Atom | Electronic structure | Electrons needed to fill outer shell |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | | |
| Carbon | | |
| Nitrogen | | |
| Oxygen | | |
| Chlorine | | |

**Homework answers:**
1. Giant ionic lattice; strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions; in all directions; a lot of energy needed to overcome these forces.
2. (a) CaF2, (b) K2O, (c) AlN
3. Mg with 2 outer electrons transferred, one to each Cl; Mg2+ shown with no outer electrons in brackets; each Cl- with 8 outer electrons in brackets and charge shown.
4. H: 1, needs 1. C: 2,4, needs 4. N: 2,5, needs 3. O: 2,6, needs 2. Cl: 2,8,7, needs 1.
