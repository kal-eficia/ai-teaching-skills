# Session plan: GCSE Chemistry, Year 10 (1 hour, online, 1:1)

**Focus this week:** fix the two gaps from last week (explaining high melting points; deriving ionic formulae rather than guessing), then bridge into covalent bonding so next week's school lessons land on solid ground.

## Assumptions
- Exam board is unspecified, so everything is pitched at the common GCSE (9-1) content shared by AQA, Edexcel and OCR. Check the spec wording for your board.
- You have a shared screen or whiteboard and can see her written work.
- Her homework (6 ionic formulae questions) has been done and you can see it. Use her answers to decide how long to spend in Part 2: if 5 or 6 are right with working, cut Part 2 to 10 minutes and give the time to Part 3.
- She has not yet met covalent bonding in school.

## Learning goals (say these to her at the start)
1. Work out any simple ionic formula from the ion charges, and say why it is that formula.
2. Explain, in a full chain of reasoning, why ionic compounds have high melting points.
3. Know what makes covalent bonding different from ionic bonding, ready for school next week.

## 1. Retrieval starter (0-8 min)
Ask without notes, one at a time, and let her answer aloud before you reveal anything:
1. What happens to the electrons when sodium bonds with chlorine? (Sodium loses one, chlorine gains one.)
2. Why does sodium form a 1+ ion? (Group 1: one outer electron; losing it gives a full outer shell.)
3. Draw the dot-and-cross diagram for MgO from memory. (Tests transfer of the NaCl skill to 2+ and 2-. Watch whether she moves two electrons.)
4. What is an ion? (A charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains electrons.)

Quick homework check: ask her to tell you which of the 6 questions felt shakiest before you look. Her confidence rating is useful data alongside her answers.

## 2. Ionic formulae: replacing guessing with a method (8-28 min)

**Why she is guessing:** she probably has formulae as memorised strings. Give her a 3-step method she can run every time.

**The method (put it on screen):**
1. **Charges:** find each ion's charge (from the group number for main-group metals and non-metals, or from the data given).
2. **Balance:** how many of each ion make the total charge zero?
3. **Write it:** put the number as a subscript after the symbol; no number means one; use brackets for compound ions.

**Worked example, MgCl2 (model it, thinking aloud):**
- Mg is Group 2, so Mg2+. Cl is Group 7, so Cl-.
- One Mg2+ gives +2. One Cl- gives only -1. Need two Cl- to cancel.
- Formula: MgCl2. Check: +2 and 2 x (-1) = 0.

**Then she does, you watch (do not rescue too fast):**
- Na2O (checks she gets 2 sodium for 1 oxide)
- AlCl3
- CaO (the trap: charges already balance, so no subscripts; a common error is writing Ca2O2)
- Al2O3 (the hardest simple one, 3+ and 2-: lowest common multiple of 6)
- Mg(OH)2 or Ca(NO3)2 (brackets for compound ions; give her OH- and NO3- charges)

**Misconceptions to listen for**
- "Criss-cross" without understanding: she swaps numbers but does not simplify (e.g. Mg2+ with O2- becomes Mg2O2 instead of MgO). Ask: "Does the formula tell us the ratio of ions? So what is the simplest ratio?"
- Writing charges as part of the formula (Mg2+Cl2-). Charges belong on the ions, not in the formula.
- Thinking the subscript 2 in MgCl2 means a molecule of two chlorines bonded to magnesium. It is a ratio in a lattice.

**Link back to her diagram skill:** ask her to draw the dot-and-cross for MgCl2. She should see two chlorine atoms each taking one electron from one magnesium. Seeing why the 2 appears from the electron transfer stops it feeling arbitrary.

**Go through her homework** with whatever time remains: for any wrong answer, ask her to re-do it with the three steps rather than telling her the answer.

## 3. Explaining high melting points (28-43 min)

**Diagnose first (2 min):** ask her to try the explanation now, out loud, and note where it stalls. Common weak answers: "because the bonds are strong" (which bonds? what actually has to happen when it melts?), or mentioning molecules.

**Build the chain with her, as a sequence of linked steps.** Put these on screen as blanks she fills in:
1. Ionic compounds are made of a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions.
2. There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions, acting in all directions throughout the lattice.
3. To melt, the ions must be able to move, so these forces must be overcome.
4. This takes a lot of energy, so the melting point is high.

**Keep the vocabulary precise:**
- Say "ions" and "giant ionic lattice", never "molecules" of NaCl.
- The forces are "electrostatic forces between ions" or "ionic bonds"; accept either, but make sure she says what is attracted to what.
- "Energy to overcome" the forces, not "energy to break the atoms".

**Practise the skill (exam style):**
- Command-word task: "Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point." (4 marks on some papers.) She writes her answer in the chat or on paper, then you compare against the four-step chain. Mark it together using the chain as a checklist.
- Extension: "Magnesium oxide melts at about 2850 degrees C; sodium chloride at about 801 degrees C. Suggest why." Guide her to ion charges: Mg2+ and O2- are more highly charged, so stronger attraction. Only push this if the first task goes smoothly; it is higher-tier thinking and good stretch.

**Quick related checks if time allows:** why do ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved but not when solid? (Ions are free to move; in the solid they are held in fixed positions.) This reuses the same lattice idea.

## 4. Bridge to covalent bonding (43-53 min)

Keep this a preview and a hook, not a full lesson. School teaches it next week; the aim is to give her a framework to attach it to.

**Hook question:** "Chlorine is a gas at room temperature, but sodium chloride is a solid with a melting point of over 800 degrees C. Both contain chlorine. What might be different about how the atoms are joined?"

**Core ideas to establish (about 10 minutes):**
- Non-metals bonding with non-metals (e.g. H and Cl, or two H atoms) do not transfer electrons. They **share** pairs of electrons.
- A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, and each atom ends up with a full outer shell.
- Quick draw: H2 (two hydrogen atoms sharing one pair), then ask her to predict HCl.
- Contrast table she fills in, with her predictions first:

| | Ionic | Covalent |
|---|---|---|
| Between | metal and non-metal | non-metal and non-metal |
| Electrons | transferred | shared |
| Particles | ions | molecules (for simple covalent) |

**Do not go further than this.** Do not explain simple molecular melting points in depth, as that is next week's school content and it is easy to teach it wrongly in a rush (the key point, that melting simple molecular substances overcomes weak intermolecular forces and does not break covalent bonds, deserves a proper slot). Tell her: "Keep a note of the melting point question. Next week at school you will be able to answer it for covalent substances and compare."

## 5. Exit check and homework (53-60 min)

**Exit check (no notes, 4 minutes):**
1. Write the formula of aluminium oxide and show your three steps.
2. In two sentences, explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point.
3. Confidence rating for each, 1 to 5.

If she gets the formula right with working and her explanation includes lattice, strong electrostatic forces, and "overcome / lots of energy", both gaps are closed. If not, note exactly which link is missing and open next session with it.

**Homework (set at the end):**
- 6 mixed formulae questions, with the method written out for each (at least two with 2+ and 3- type ions, one with brackets).
- One 4-mark "explain why" question on ionic melting points, written in full.
- Optional: draw dot-and-cross diagrams for H2 and HCl and bring questions about covalent bonding from school.

## Teacher notes
- **Shift confidence, not just accuracy:** her saying she was "guessing" is the real signal. Praise the method, not just right answers, and have her say the three steps aloud each time.
- **Follow-up:** next session, spend the first 5 minutes on a one-question retrieval of ionic melting points, spaced from this week, then link it to the covalent comparison from school.
- **Resources to have ready:** a periodic table with group numbers, a table of common compound-ion charges (OH-, NO3-, SO4 2-, CO3 2-, NH4+), and a diagram of a sodium chloride lattice.
