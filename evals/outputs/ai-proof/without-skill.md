# Setting an AI-resistant history homework on the causes of WW1

**Assumptions:** UK Year 9, so roughly 13 to 14 year olds, mixed ability, and you want homework that takes about 45 to 60 minutes. I've assumed you want to keep the same content (causes of WW1) but change the task. Adjust as needed.

## The short answer

You can't make an essay ChatGPT-proof. "Explain the causes of WW1" is one of the most common essay prompts on the internet, so any AI will produce a competent answer in seconds. The fix is to stop trying to police the tool and change what you're asking for. Set tasks where the thinking has to be visible, personal, or happen in front of you.

Here are the options, roughly from least to most disruptive to your current plan.

## Option 1: Keep the essay, but make the prompt specific and personal to your lessons

Generic prompts get generic AI answers. Tie the question to things only your class has done.

**Example:**
> In our lessons we looked at the Sarajevo assassination, the alliance system and the naval race. Choose the ONE cause you think mattered most. Using at least two sources from our lesson pack (name them), argue why it matters more than the other two. You must include one point you now disagree with from the class discussion on Tuesday, and say why.

Why it works: the AI doesn't know your lesson pack, your sources or your class debate. It will either invent them (which is easy to spot) or write something bland.

Add these constraints to make it harder still:
- Require named sources from your own pack, with a short quote.
- Ask for a judgement ("which mattered most and why"), not a description ("what were the causes").
- Ask for one sentence on what they found hardest or changed their mind about.

## Option 2: Make it a ranking and justification task (my top recommendation)

**Diamond nine or ranking task:**
Give pupils 9 cause cards (militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism, the Balkans, the Kaiser's ambitions, the Schlieffen Plan, the assassination, the arms race). They:
1. Arrange them in a diamond, most important at the top.
2. Write 3 to 4 sentences justifying their top choice and 3 to 4 justifying their bottom choice.
3. Write one sentence on how two of the causes are linked ("Alliances turned a local crisis into a European one because...").

Why it works: it is short, it's about judgement, and the diamond is unique to each pupil. Doing it on paper or a photo of a hand-drawn diamond makes it easy to check. It also teaches what history teachers actually want: weighing and linking causes, not listing them.

## Option 3: Do the thinking in class, use homework to prepare

Flip it. Homework becomes the preparation, and the assessed part happens in the room.

- **Homework:** "Bring three bullet points on why you think the war started, and one question you still have." Low stakes, takes 15 minutes, and it doesn't matter if AI helps because it isn't the assessed piece.
- **In class (20 to 30 minutes, handwritten, no devices):** Pupils write the essay or a structured paragraph from their notes.

This is the most reliable option if you need a trustworthy piece of work for assessment. The homework then does its proper job, which is practice and preparation.

## Option 4: Oral or recorded explanation

- Pupils record a 90-second voice note or video: "Explain to a younger pupil why the war started. Use one analogy."
- Or, in the next lesson, pick pupils at random and ask them to explain their answer for 60 seconds ("cold call").

AI can write a script, but it's much harder to fake understanding when you ask a follow-up question on the spot. Even a handful of random follow-ups makes pupils take the homework seriously.

## Option 5: Use the AI on purpose (critique task)

Since they will use it anyway, build it into the task and make it a thinking exercise.

> Ask an AI tool: "Explain the causes of WW1." Paste in the answer. Then:
> 1. Highlight one thing it said that you can back up from our lessons.
> 2. Find one thing that is missing, oversimplified or wrong.
> 3. Write a paragraph rewriting its weakest section so it is better. Explain what you changed and why.

Why it works: it is honest about the reality, it teaches critical evaluation (an important skill), and it is hard to cheat because the marks are for the pupil's own judgement. A word of caution: some pupils will ask AI to do the critique as well, so keep the marks weighted towards the specific, class-linked bits (point 1, and the "why" in point 3).

## Option 6: Source-based task with unseen material

Give a short extract the AI won't have a ready-made answer for, such as a source from your lessons, a cartoon, or a short letter from 1914. Ask:

> Read the source. What does it suggest about why people supported the war? Is it a reliable guide to the causes of the war overall? Explain using the source and your own knowledge.

Include the source in the homework itself (printed or as a PDF). Pupils can paste it into ChatGPT, but the question is narrower, and you can ask them to quote exact words and refer to details only visible in the source.

## Practical tips whichever option you choose

1. **Tell them why.** Say plainly: "The point of this task is to practise your own thinking, because that is what the exam tests." Pupils cheat less when they see a reason beyond the grade.
2. **Mark the thinking, not the polish.** Give more marks for a clear judgement, linked causes and use of evidence from your lessons, and fewer for fluent writing.
3. **Know their voice.** A short low-stakes baseline piece written in class lets you spot work that suddenly sounds very different.
4. **Don't rely on AI detectors.** They are unreliable and produce false accusations, especially for pupils with weaker English or SEN. Use a quick conversation instead: "Talk me through your second paragraph."
5. **Ask for process evidence.** A photo of rough notes, a plan or the diamond nine is quick to check and hard to fake convincingly.
6. **Check your school's policy.** Many schools now have an AI use policy. Aligning with it protects you if you need to follow up on a concern.

## If I had to pick one

Use **Option 2 (ranking and justification)** as the homework, then spend 10 minutes at the start of the next lesson on **Option 4**: cold-call three or four pupils to defend their top choice. It takes little extra planning, it's better history teaching than the original essay, and a pupil who let ChatGPT do it will struggle to explain it live.
