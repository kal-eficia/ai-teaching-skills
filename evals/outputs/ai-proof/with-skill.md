# Redesigning "Write an essay explaining the causes of the First World War"

## Diagnosis

If a student pastes this into ChatGPT, they skip almost everything: recalling the causes, deciding which mattered most, linking cause to effect, and building an argument. The task asks for a product that AI produces in seconds, and the topic is so well covered that the output will be competent and generic. So the fix is to change the task rather than to ban AI or try to detect it.

## Assumptions

- Year 9, so about 13 to 14 years old. This is a one-week homework, with a short in-class component.
- You have taught the main causes in class (militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism, the assassination at Sarajevo and the July Crisis). I've used the common "MAIN" framework, so swap in whatever you taught.
- Students have a class set of notes or a textbook, and some have home access to AI.
- You can spare about 5 minutes of lesson time and a few minutes per student for a spot check. If not, use the "no live check" option at the end.

## The redesigned task (student-facing)

### Why did Europe go to war in 1914? Rank the causes

**Rules for this task: AI to check.** Do each stage yourself first. Then you may use AI, but only where the task says so. Show me both versions.

**Stage 1: From memory (no notes, no devices, 10 minutes, done in class or at the start of homework)**
Write down every cause of the First World War you can remember, in your own words. Then pick the one you think mattered most and write 3 to 4 sentences on why. Hand this in or photograph it. It will not be marked for mistakes.

**Stage 2: Build your ranking (notes and textbook allowed, no AI)**
1. Choose the **four** causes you think were most important and rank them from 1 (most important) to 4.
2. For each one, write **two sentences**: what it was, and how it pushed Europe towards war.
3. Use **at least one fact from our lessons** for each cause, such as a date, a name, a treaty or a source we looked at. Use at least **six of these key words** across your answer: *alliance, arms race, empire, nationalism, ultimatum, mobilisation, Schlieffen Plan, July Crisis*.
4. Write a **150-word paragraph** (exactly between 140 and 160 words) explaining why your number 1 cause outranks your number 2. Start with: "I think ... mattered more than ... because ..."

**Stage 3: Catch the AI out (AI to check)**
Your teacher has given you the answer an AI wrote to this question (see the attached sheet). It looks convincing, but it has problems.
1. Find **three** weaknesses. These might be a wrong or missing fact, a cause that is listed but not explained, something that skips what we learned in class, or a ranking with no reasons.
2. For each one, say what is wrong and write a corrected version.
3. Then write **two sentences**: what does the AI not seem to understand about *why* these causes mattered?

**Stage 4: Your own AI check (optional but encouraged)**
Paste your Stage 2 ranking into an AI and ask: "What have I missed, and where is my reasoning weak?" Copy your prompt and its reply. Then write **three bullet points**: one thing you changed because of it, one thing you rejected and why, and one thing you checked against our notes.

**Stage 5: Explain it (next lesson, 90 seconds)**
I'll ask you to explain your number 1 cause out loud, without notes, and answer one follow-up question, such as "What would have happened if that cause hadn't existed?"

### Teacher prep: the AI answer for Stage 3
Generate it yourself with a prompt like "Write a Year 9 answer on the causes of WW1." Then check it and tweak it to include three planted weaknesses. Ideas that work well:
- one slightly wrong date or detail
- a cause named but never explained
- a confident claim with no evidence
- no ranking or judgement at all
- nothing about the specific sources or events from your lessons

Because you control the flaws, you know exactly what a good critique should find. You can also reuse the same sheet for the whole class.

## What students submit

1. Stage 1 memory sheet (photo or paper)
2. Stage 2 ranking, evidence and 150-word paragraph
3. Stage 3 critique with corrections
4. Stage 4 prompt, AI reply and three-bullet reflection (if they used AI)
5. Stage 5 oral explanation (in class)

The final "essay" is gone. The thinking is the submission.

## Marking guide (20 marks)

| Part | Marks | What earns credit |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: memory | 2 | Completed honestly. Marked for effort, not accuracy. Gives you a baseline of what they actually know. |
| Stage 2: ranking and evidence | 6 | Four relevant causes. A sensible ranking. Each cause linked to war (2 marks), class-specific facts used (2 marks), key words used correctly (2 marks). |
| Stage 2: 150-word paragraph | 4 | Within the word limit. Compares two causes directly. Gives a reason, not just a description. |
| Stage 3: critique | 4 | Finds real weaknesses (not made-up ones). Corrections are accurate. Shows some sense of what the AI missed (1 mark for the final two sentences). |
| Stage 4: AI check reflection | 2 | Shows judgement: they changed something, rejected something and said why. Full marks even if they chose not to use AI but explained what they did instead. |
| Stage 5: oral explanation | 2 | Can explain their top cause in their own words and answer a follow-up. |

Credit thinking over polish. A clumsy but personal ranking with real class evidence should beat a smooth answer with no evidence. If a student's written work is far stronger than their Stage 1 and Stage 5, that gap is your conversation to have, and the oral check is what protects you.

## Why this redesign protects the learning

Students have to recall, rank and defend causes themselves before AI is allowed anywhere near the task, and they then use AI as something to judge rather than something to copy.

## Lighter option if time is tight

Keep Stages 1, 2 and 5 only: a memory write-up, a ranked list with class evidence and a word-limited paragraph, and a quick explain-it check. This takes about a third of the setup effort and still beats the original essay.

## No live check available?

Replace Stage 5 with a 60-second voice recording or a short written "teach it to a friend" note submitted with the work, then spot-check a few at random in class.
