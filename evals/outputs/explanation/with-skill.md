# Why we have seasons: Year 7 explanation (ages 11 to 12)

**Assumptions (no teaching-context file used):** UK class, northern hemisphere, no prior knowledge beyond knowing the Earth orbits the Sun and spins once a day. Needs one torch, one globe or a ball/orange with a pencil through it, and a sheet of paper. About 10 minutes with the demo.

**The big idea to land:** Seasons are caused by the **tilt** of the Earth's axis, not by distance. The Earth is actually *closest* to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the UK's winter.

---

## 1. Teacher script (about 3 minutes spoken)

**Hook to what they know**

"Put your hand near a campfire. Now stand further back. Warmer when you're close, right? So lots of you think summer happens because the Earth moves closer to the Sun. That's a really sensible idea. It's also wrong, and I'm going to prove it."

**Concrete example first (torch and paper)**

*Demo: hold a torch about 20 cm above a sheet of paper, pointing straight down.*

"Look at the circle of light. Small, bright, strong. Now I tilt the torch, same distance away. What happens to the light?"

*(Take answers: it stretches out, gets dimmer.)*

"Same torch, same distance, same amount of light. But when it hits at a slant, the light is spread over a bigger area. So each bit of ground gets less energy. Straight-on light heats up the ground more. Slanted light heats it less."

**Then the abstraction (key words to define)**

"The Earth's **axis** is the imaginary line through the North and South Poles that it spins around. This axis is not upright. It is **tilted** by 23.5 degrees. As the Earth travels once around the Sun, which takes a year, the axis keeps pointing the same way in space. So for part of the year the northern half leans *towards* the Sun, and for part of the year it leans *away*."

*Demo: walk the globe round the "Sun" (a student holding the torch, or the torch on a desk). Keep the pencil pointing at the same corner of the room all the way round.*

"When the UK leans towards the Sun:
- the Sun is higher in the sky, so light hits us more directly (like the straight torch)
- days are longer, so we get more hours of heating

That's **summer**. When the UK leans away:
- the Sun is low in the sky, so light hits at a slant (like the tilted torch)
- days are shorter

That's **winter**. Spring and autumn are the in-between points."

**Second, different example (it transfers)**

"Here's a test of the idea. When it's summer in the UK, it's winter in Australia. If distance was the cause, the whole Earth would get summer at once, because we're all about the same distance from the Sun. Tilt explains it: when our half leans towards the Sun, their half leans away."

*Optional extra: near the Equator the Sun is high all year, so there is hardly any seasonal change.*

**Non-example (the thing that looks similar but isn't)**

"The distance idea feels right because *getting closer to a fire* does warm you up. But the Earth's orbit is only a very slightly squashed circle. The Earth is closest to the Sun around 3 January, about 147 million km away. It's furthest around 4 July, about 152 million km. That's only about 3% different, and the closest point is in our winter. So distance can't be the cause."

**Check question** (see section 3), then wrap up with the one-line summary: "Tilt, not distance."

---

## 2. The analogy, and where it breaks

**Analogy:** A torch shining on a tilted ball (the globe). Shine a torch on the tilted globe: the part tilted towards the torch gets a bright, concentrated patch. The part tilted away gets a stretched, dim patch. That is the Earth and the Sun.

**Where it breaks (say this out loud):**
1. **In the paper demo we tilt the torch, but in real life the Sun doesn't tilt or move up and down.** It is the *ground* that sits at a slant to the Sun's rays, because of where you are on the curved, tilted Earth. Say this so students don't think the Sun itself moves.
2. **The Earth's axis doesn't wobble back and forth during the year.** It keeps pointing the same way (towards roughly the same star, Polaris). It is the Earth's *position* in its orbit that changes which half leans towards the Sun. With the globe, keep the pencil fixed pointing at one corner of the room.
3. **The Earth is not a hot ball you stand next to.** The "closer to a fire" feeling is exactly the thing that causes the misconception, so name it.

**Diagram to draw (for the board):** The Sun in the centre. The Earth drawn four times around it (June, September, December, March), each with the axis tilted in the *same direction* (all pointing to the same corner of the board). Label June "UK leans towards Sun: summer" and December "UK leans away: winter". Do not draw the orbit as a long squashed oval, or students will take that as proof of the distance idea.

---

## 3. Check question

**Question:** "Someone says: 'It's hot in July because the Earth is closer to the Sun then.' Give two pieces of evidence that show they are wrong."

**Strong answer (any two):**
- The Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January, which is winter in the UK.
- When it is summer in the UK it is winter in Australia, but we are the same distance from the Sun.
- The seasons are caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis (23.5 degrees), which changes how directly the Sun's light hits us and how long the days are.

**Most likely wrong answers:**
1. "The tilt makes us closer to the Sun in summer." (Tilt is being used, but they've kept the distance idea. Reply: "Tilt changes the *angle* of the light and the *length of the day*, not how far away the Sun is. The distance changes by only about 3%.")
2. "The Earth tilts towards and away from the Sun as it goes round." (They think the axis wobbles. Reply: "Look at the globe again. The pencil always points the same way. It's the Earth's *position* that changes.")
3. "The Earth spinning makes the seasons." (Spinning gives day and night. Going round the Sun, with the tilt, gives seasons.)

---

## 4. Student version (to read later or stick in books)

**Why do we have seasons?**

Many people think summer happens because the Earth is closer to the Sun. This is not true. In fact, the Earth is closest to the Sun in January, which is winter in the UK.

Seasons happen because the Earth's **axis** (the imaginary line through the North and South Poles) is **tilted** by 23.5 degrees. As the Earth travels around the Sun over one year, the axis always points the same way. This means that for half the year the northern half of the Earth leans towards the Sun, and for the other half it leans away.

When the UK leans towards the Sun, sunlight hits us more directly and the days are longer, so we get more heat: summer. When the UK leans away, sunlight hits us at a slant, so the same light is spread over a bigger area, and the days are shorter: winter. At the same time, Australia has the opposite season to us.

**Remember: tilt, not distance.**

**Key words:** axis, tilt, orbit, hemisphere, direct light, daylight hours.
