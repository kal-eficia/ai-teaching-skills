# Session plan: Year 10 GCSE Chemistry (60 minutes, online)

**Focus this week:** close the two gaps from last week (explaining high melting points, and formulae she "guesses"), then give her a small, clean bridge into covalent bonding so next week's school lessons land on solid ground.

**Assumptions I've made**
- She's on a combined or separate science course (AQA/Edexcel style). Compound ions (sulfate, hydroxide etc.) are treated as a stretch, not core.
- You can share a screen or a digital whiteboard, and she can type or write on camera.
- Her "guessing" on MgCl2 means she doesn't have a method, not that she lacks the knowledge. So the fix is a method she can use every time, not more memorising.

**By the end she should be able to**
1. Write a full, correct explanation of why ionic compounds have high melting points, without prompts.
2. Work out an ionic formula from the periodic table in three steps, and say why it works.
3. Say in one sentence how covalent bonding differs from ionic bonding.

## 0-5 min: Retrieval starter

No notes, no hints. Ask these one at a time and let her think for a few seconds before answering.

1. What happens to the electrons when sodium bonds with chlorine?
2. What charge does a sodium ion have, and why?
3. Why is magnesium oxide described as a giant structure rather than a molecule?
4. What is the formula of sodium chloride?

**What you're listening for:** whether she says "ions" and "electrostatic attraction" unprompted. Note which questions she hesitates on. You'll use that in the next section.

*Why start this way:* getting answers out of her memory is a better test of learning than looking over notes, and it shows you honestly where she is after a week.

---

## 5-20 min: Homework review as a diagnosis

Don't just mark the six questions. Pick the two she found hardest (she'll know) and ask her to talk through her working aloud: "How did you decide on that formula?"

Listen for which of these is happening:
- She doesn't know the ion charges from the group number.
- She knows the charges but isn't sure how to combine them.
- She does it right but doesn't trust it (a confidence issue).

**Teach the three-step method** (put it on screen and leave it up for the rest of the session):

1. **Find the charge of each ion** from the group number. Group 1 is 1+, group 2 is 2+, group 3 is 3+. Group 6 is 2-, group 7 is 1-.
2. **Balance the charges** so the total positive equals the total negative.
3. **Write the formula** with the smallest whole-number ratio of ions.

**Worked example, MgCl2** (do it with her, don't just show it):
- Mg is in group 2, so Mg2+. Cl is in group 7, so Cl-.
- One Mg2+ gives 2+. One Cl- gives 1-. She needs two Cl- to cancel the 2+.
- Formula: MgCl2. The "2" is there because the charges have to cancel, not because someone decided it.

**Check she's got it:** ask her to explain why the formula for magnesium oxide is MgO and not MgO2. This is the key question. If she can say "Mg2+ and O2- already balance", the idea has landed.

**Common error to watch for:** writing the charge as the subscript (for example Mg2Cl1). Name it directly: "The charge tells you how many electrons moved. The subscript tells you how many ions you need."

## 20-37 min: Why ionic compounds have high melting points

This is the biggest gap, so give it the most time. Build the explanation in layers rather than giving her a model answer to memorise.

**Step 1: Picture it (3 min).** Show a diagram of the NaCl lattice (she can already draw the dot-and-cross, so link it: "That diagram shows one bond. Now zoom out"). Ask her: "What do you notice about how the ions are arranged?" Draw out: a regular, repeating, giant structure, with every ion surrounded by oppositely charged ions.

**Step 2: Ask the key question (2 min).** "When a solid melts, what actually has to happen to the particles?" Many students think bonds "break" in a vague sense, or that molecules separate. Make it clear: the ions have to gain enough energy to move out of their fixed positions. And there are no molecules in an ionic compound, only a lattice.

**Step 3: Build the chain together (5 min).** Put these sentence starters on screen and have her complete each:

- Ionic compounds have a giant lattice of ...
- The ions are held together by strong ...
- These forces act in ... directions throughout the lattice.
- So a lot of ... is needed to overcome them.
- Therefore the melting point is ...

**Model answer (reveal only after she's tried):**
> Ionic compounds have a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions. There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the ions, and these act in all directions throughout the structure. A lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces, so ionic compounds have high melting points.

**Step 4: Fade the support (5 min).** Delete the sentence starters. Ask her to write the explanation again, in her own words, in the chat or on paper held to the camera. Then compare it with the model, tick each key idea she included, and name the one she missed.

**The four marking-scheme ideas to tick:**
1. Giant lattice / structure of ions
2. Strong electrostatic forces of attraction (between oppositely charged ions)
3. Lots of energy needed to overcome them
4. So high melting point

**Misconceptions to head off:**
- "The ionic bond breaks." Better: the electrostatic attraction between ions is overcome. Nothing about the bonds inside an ion is involved.
- "It's hard to break the molecule." There are no molecules here.
- Don't mix this up with covalent substances. This matters next week (see the bridge below).

**Quick extension if time allows (2 min):** ask why molten sodium chloride conducts electricity but solid sodium chloride doesn't. (The ions are free to move when molten.) It uses the same idea and she'll need it anyway.

---

## 37-47 min: Formulae practice with fading support

Keep the three-step method visible for the first two, then take it away.

**With the method on screen:**
- Sodium oxide (Na+ and O2-): **Na2O**
- Calcium chloride (Ca2+ and Cl-): **CaCl2**

**Method hidden, she explains aloud as she goes:**
- Aluminium oxide (Al3+ and O2-): **Al2O3**. This is a good one: the "criss-cross" shortcut works, but make her explain that 6+ balances 6-.
- Lithium sulfide (Li+ and S2-): **Li2S**
- Magnesium nitride is too hard for now, so skip it unless she's flying.

**Mixed in, to stop her pattern-matching:** one question where the answer needs no subscripts, for example potassium chloride (**KCl**), and one where she has to work backwards: "The formula is CaF2. What charge does fluoride have, and how do you know?"

**Stretch (only if she's confident):** compound ions, using the ion charges given in the question, for example magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)2. Explain why the brackets are needed.

**Watch for:** her saying "I think it's..." That's the guessing habit. Ask "What's your evidence?" and have her point to the charges.

---

## 47-55 min: Bridge to covalent bonding

School starts covalent bonding next week, so aim for a short, clear contrast rather than teaching the topic. Prime her, don't pre-empt her teacher.

**Show a two-column comparison and fill it in with her:**

| | Ionic | Covalent |
|---|---|---|
| Between | Metal and non-metal | Non-metals only |
| What happens to electrons | Transferred | Shared |
| Particles formed | Ions | Molecules (mostly) |
| Held together by | Electrostatic attraction between ions | Shared pairs of electrons |

**One prediction question:** "Chlorine and carbon are both non-metals. Do you think they'd transfer electrons or share them? Why?" Her answer doesn't have to be perfect. It makes her think before school teaches it.

**Plant this for later:** "When we look at melting points of simple covalent substances, you'll see they're usually low. Think about why that might be, given what you now know about ionic ones." Don't answer it. It builds a hook, and the contrast between the two lattices and molecules becomes a very common exam question.

**Quickly draw a dot-and-cross for hydrogen (H2)** and ask what's different from NaCl. That gives her a first picture of sharing to build on.

---

## 55-60 min: Exit check and homework

**Exit check (no notes, 3 minutes):**
1. Write the formula for calcium oxide and explain how you got it. (CaO: Ca2+ and O2- balance.)
2. Explain in two or three sentences why magnesium oxide has a high melting point.

Compare to the marking points above. If she gets both right, say so specifically ("you named the electrostatic forces and said they act in all directions, which is exactly what examiners want"). If one slips, don't re-teach now. Note it and start next week's retrieval with it.

**Homework (about 20 minutes, spread across the week):**
1. **Formulae (5 questions).** Write the ions first, then the formula, then a one-line "why". Mix in one where she works backwards from the formula to the charge.
2. **Explain it again (6 marks style):** "Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point." Write it from memory first, then check against the model.
3. **Spaced retrieval:** three quick questions from ionic bonding last week that she hasn't looked at since (for example, what is an ion, and how does a metal atom become an ion?).
4. **Pre-read:** one line on what she thinks "covalent" means, before school teaches it. Nothing formal.

Suggest she does the formulae over two days rather than one go. A bit of forgetting in between helps the learning stick.

---

## Notes for you

- **If she's quicker than expected:** push on to why ionic compounds are brittle (layers shift, like charges line up and repel), or the conductivity question. Both reuse the same lattice idea.
- **If she's slower:** cut the stretch and the covalent comparison down to the table alone. The melting point explanation and the formula method are the priorities; the bridge is a bonus.
- **Next week's opener:** a short retrieval quiz mixing ionic formulae, the melting point explanation, and one covalent question from her school lesson. Then you can see how the two topics sit together in her head.
- **Confidence:** her dot-and-cross diagram was correct, so she can do the hard visual part. Say that to her. The "guessing" feeling is likely about trusting a method, and a method she can see herself using will help that more than reassurance.
