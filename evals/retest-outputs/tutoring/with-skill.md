## 1. Retrieval starter (8 min)

**Rules for S:** no notes, answer from memory, and rate each answer 1 (guessing), 2 (fairly sure) or 3 (certain) before you reveal the answer. Do not re-explain anything yet. Just note where each answer breaks. That tells you how to use the rest of the session.

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point. (3 marks, say it aloud or type it) | Giant ionic lattice (1). Strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions, in all directions (1). A lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces (1). |
| 2 | Magnesium atoms have 2 outer electrons. What ion do they form, and why? | Mg2+. It loses 2 electrons to get a full outer shell. |
| 3 | Chlorine is in Group 7. What ion does it form? | Cl-. It gains 1 electron. |
| 4 | So why is magnesium chloride MgCl2 and not MgCl? | One Mg2+ has a charge of +2. Each Cl- is -1, so you need two Cl- to balance it. Total charge is zero. |
| 5 | What is the formula of potassium sulfide? (K in Group 1, S in Group 6) | K2S. K+ and S2-, so two K+ per S2-. |
| 6 | Older topic: how many protons, neutrons and electrons are in an atom of 23Na (atomic number 11)? | 11 protons, 12 neutrons, 11 electrons. |

**What to listen for in question 1:** the usual breakages are "ionic bonds are weak" (wrong), "the atoms melt" or "molecules separate" (there are no molecules in an ionic compound), or a bare "because it has strong bonds" with no mention of ions, attraction or energy. Write down exactly what she says.

**Branch for questions 2 to 5:**
- If she rates 3 on most and gets them right, move on.
- If she rates 1 or gets question 4 or 5 wrong, give the 5-minute repair below at the start of the teach stage, take 5 minutes off independent practice (drop Q3), and keep the explanation work as the main focus.

**5-minute formula repair (only if needed):**
1. Write both ions with their charges: Mg2+ and Cl-.
2. Ask: how many of each do I need so the total charge is zero?
3. Write the formula with those numbers as subscripts. Leave out any 1.
4. Try Na2O (Na+ and O2-, so Na2O) and CaF2 (Ca2+ and F-, so CaF2). She explains each aloud before you confirm.

## 2. Homework review (5 min)

Don't re-mark it in silence. Say: "Pick the one you were least sure of and tell me how you got it."

- Listen for whether she starts from charges (good) or from a remembered pattern (fragile).
- Ask her for one answer she thinks is right, and why. Then ask: "How would you check it?" The check is that the charges add up to zero.
- If she says "I just guessed" on any answer, redo that one together using the ion-charge method on screen.

---

## 3. Teach: the three-link chain (15 min)

**Today's single focus:** to explain a property you link structure, forces and energy.

**Step 1: See the structure (3 min).** Sketch a 2D slice of the NaCl lattice: alternating Na+ and Cl- in a grid. Ask her to tell you what she notices. Draw out: it carries on in all directions, and every ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions. Label it "giant ionic lattice".

**Step 2: The chain (4 min).** Put these three prompts on screen:

1. **What is it made of?** Name the structure and the particles. (A giant lattice of oppositely charged ions.)
2. **What holds the particles together?** Name the forces and say how strong. (Strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions.)
3. **What has to happen when it melts?** Say what must be overcome and what that needs. (These forces must be overcome, which takes a lot of energy, so the melting point is high.)

Say out loud: "Every property question is these three links. Next week's covalent questions use the same three prompts with different answers."

**Step 3: Model it (4 min).** Think aloud as you write the full answer for NaCl.

> Sodium chloride has a giant ionic lattice of oppositely charged ions. There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the ions in all directions. A lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces, so it has a high melting point.

Point out the wording choices: "ions", "electrostatic forces of attraction", "overcome", "a lot of energy". These are the words the mark scheme looks for. Name the two common slips: writing "molecules" or "atoms", and writing "the bonds are strong" without saying between what.

**Step 4: Check understanding (2 min).** Ask:
- "Why can't I say 'a molecule of sodium chloride melts'?" (Ionic compounds are a giant lattice. There are no separate molecules.)
- "Is it the ionic compound's atoms or its ions that attract?" (Ions.)

**Step 5: Quick link back to formulae (2 min).** Point at the lattice sketch. For MgCl2, redraw with one Mg2+ for every two Cl-. "The formula is the simplest ratio of ions in the lattice. That's why the charges have to balance." This ties the two gaps together without adding a second teaching point.

## 4. Guided practice (10 min)

**Question:** Explain why magnesium oxide has a high melting point. (3 marks)

Give the sentence frame on screen. She fills it in and you prompt only when she's stuck.

> Magnesium oxide has a giant ______ lattice of ______ charged ______. There are strong ______ forces of ______ between the ______. A lot of ______ is needed to ______ these forces, so the melting point is ______.

Prompts, to be used only if needed, in this order:
1. "Which of the three links are you on?"
2. "What particles are in it? Atoms, molecules or ions?"
3. "What have you said about the forces? Strong or weak? Between what?"

**Answer:** giant ionic lattice of oppositely charged ions (Mg2+ and O2-); strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the ions; a lot of energy needed to overcome these forces, so high melting point.

**Then (3 min):** remove the frame. Ask her to write the same answer for calcium chloride, CaCl2, with no prompts. Before she writes, she writes the ions (Ca2+ and Cl-) and the formula check herself.

---

## 5. Independent practice (15 min)

She works alone, with the camera on her page if possible. Watch the method: does she write the three links in order, does she use "ions" and "electrostatic", and does she start from charges for the formula?

**Q1 (3 marks).** Explain why potassium bromide has a high melting point.

*Answer:* giant ionic lattice of oppositely charged ions (K+ and Br-) (1); strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the ions (1); a lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces (1).

**Q2 (3 marks).** Solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity. Molten sodium chloride does. Explain why.

*Answer:* in the solid, the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice (1) and so cannot move (1). When molten, the ions are free to move and carry the charge (1).

**Q3 (4 marks, stretch).** Magnesium oxide melts at 2852 °C. Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. Explain why magnesium oxide has the higher melting point.

*Answer:* both have strong electrostatic forces of attraction between ions (1); in MgO the ions have a higher charge (2+ and 2-, compared with 1+ and 1-) (1); so the attraction between the ions is stronger (1); so more energy is needed to overcome the forces (1).

(If she is short of time, drop Q3 first. Treat Q3 as a bonus if she handles Q1 and Q2 cleanly.)

**Marking talk (not silent marking):** after each answer, ask her to point to the mark for "ions", "forces of attraction" and "energy". Where she misses one, she rewrites that sentence rather than being told it.

## 6. Exit check (5 min)

Closed notes.

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Explain why lithium fluoride has a high melting point. (3 marks) | Giant ionic lattice of oppositely charged ions (Li+ and F-); strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the ions; a lot of energy needed to overcome these forces. |
| 2 | Write the formula of aluminium chloride. Show your working. (Al3+, Cl-) | AlCl3. Three Cl- (3 x -1) balance one Al3+ (+3). |
| 3 | Thinking ahead, not marked: methane, CH4, melts at -182 °C. What do you think is different about what holds its particles together? | Any sensible prediction is fine. Aim for "weaker forces" or "it's not made of ions". You are planting the question for next week. |

**Decision rule:** if she gets Q1 and Q2 right without prompting, next week's covalent unit can lean on the three-link chain straight away. If she misses either, they go back into next session's retrieval starter, not into a re-teach.

## 7. Set homework (2 min)

About 20 minutes, to do alone, spaced over the week rather than in one go.

1. (3 marks) Explain why calcium oxide has a high melting point.
   *Answer:* giant ionic lattice of oppositely charged ions; strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the ions; a lot of energy needed to overcome these forces.
2. (3 marks) Explain why molten magnesium chloride conducts electricity.
   *Answer:* it contains ions; in the molten state the ions are free to move; they carry the electrical charge.
3. Write the formula of each compound. For every one, show the ions and their charges first:
   a) lithium oxide: Li2O
   b) magnesium bromide: MgBr2
   c) aluminium oxide: Al2O3 (Al3+, O2-: two Al3+ gives +6, three O2- gives -6)
   d) calcium sulfide: CaS
   e) sodium sulfide: Na2S
4. (Preview, no marks) Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for NaCl from memory and, underneath, write one sentence saying what is different about how the atoms are joined in a molecule like methane. Wrong answers are fine here; we'll start from them next week.

Tell her: "If you catch yourself guessing a formula, stop and write the ions with their charges. The method does the work."
