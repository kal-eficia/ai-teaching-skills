# Why We Have Seasons (Year 7)

**Assumptions:** a single lesson of about 30 to 40 minutes, a globe or a ball, a torch and a lamp or window light are available, and the class has met the idea that the Earth orbits the Sun but hasn't studied tilt before.

## The big idea in one sentence

**Seasons happen because the Earth is tilted, not because it moves closer to or further from the Sun.**

## Start by taking the common idea seriously

Many of you think summer happens because we're closer to the Sun. It's a sensible guess. When you stand near a fire, you feel hotter, so why wouldn't the Earth work the same way?

Here's the problem with it, and it's a good one to test:

1. **The Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January.** That's in the middle of the UK's winter. We're about 147 million km away then.
2. **The Earth is furthest from the Sun in early July.** That's the middle of our summer. We're about 152 million km away.
3. **The difference is small.** It's about 5 million km out of roughly 150 million km. That's only about 3 per cent, which is far too little to change the weather much.
4. **Australia is having summer when we're having winter.** If distance caused the seasons, the whole planet would have summer at the same time. It doesn't, so distance can't be the cause.

Point 4 is the knockout. Ask the class: "Could distance explain Christmas on a hot beach in Sydney?" It can't.

## The real reason: the tilt

The Earth spins on an imaginary line called its **axis**, running from the North Pole to the South Pole. That axis isn't upright. It's **tilted by about 23.5 degrees**.

The key thing is that **the tilt always points the same way in space** (towards the same distant star) as the Earth travels around the Sun over a year. It doesn't wobble back and forth to follow the Sun.

That means:

- For part of the year, the **Northern Hemisphere is leaning towards the Sun**. The UK gets summer.
- Six months later, on the other side of the Sun, the **Northern Hemisphere is leaning away from the Sun**. The UK gets winter.
- The Southern Hemisphere does the exact opposite, which is why Australia's seasons are the other way round to ours.

## Why does leaning towards the Sun make it warmer?

Two things happen at the same time.

### 1. The Sun's light hits more directly

When our part of the Earth leans towards the Sun, sunlight arrives at a steep angle, almost from straight above. The light is **concentrated** onto a small patch of ground, so that patch heats up well.

When we lean away, the same sunlight arrives at a shallow, slanted angle. It gets **spread out** over a much bigger patch of ground, so each bit of ground gets less heat.

**Try this with a torch:** Shine a torch straight down onto a piece of paper. You get a small, bright circle. Now tilt the torch so the beam hits at a slant. The light stretches into a bigger, dimmer oval. Same torch, same amount of light, but it's much weaker per square centimetre. That's winter sunlight.

### 2. The days are longer in summer

When we lean towards the Sun, our part of the world spends more time in sunlight each day. In the UK in June, the Sun is up for around 16 to 17 hours. In December, it's only about 8.

More hours of sunshine and stronger sunshine means the ground and air heat up. In winter it's the other way round: weaker sunshine for fewer hours, so things cool down.

## A quick demonstration for the class

You'll need a lamp (the Sun) and a ball or globe (the Earth).

1. Put the lamp in the middle of a table.
2. Tilt the globe so the North Pole leans in a fixed direction, for example towards a particular poster on the wall.
3. Walk the globe slowly around the lamp, **keeping the tilt pointing at the same poster the whole time**.
4. Stop at four points. At each one, ask: "Is the UK leaning towards the lamp or away from it? Is the light hitting it straight on or at a slant?"

You'll see that at one side of the lamp the UK leans in (summer), on the opposite side it leans away (winter), and in between there are two points where neither half leans towards the Sun more than the other. Those are **spring and autumn**.

## The four seasons at a glance

| Time of year (UK) | What the Earth is doing | Season in UK | Season in Australia |
|---|---|---|---|
| About 21 June | Northern Hemisphere tilted most towards the Sun | Summer (longest day) | Winter |
| About 23 September | Neither half tilted towards the Sun | Autumn | Spring |
| About 21 December | Northern Hemisphere tilted most away from the Sun | Winter (shortest day) | Summer |
| About 20 March | Neither half tilted towards the Sun | Spring | Autumn |

## Common mix-ups to watch for

- **"Seasons come from distance."** As covered above, the Earth is closest to the Sun during our winter.
- **"The tilt changes during the year."** The tilt stays pointing the same way. What changes is where the Earth is in its orbit, so a different half leans towards the Sun.
- **"Day and night and seasons are caused by the same thing."** Day and night come from the Earth **spinning** once every 24 hours. Seasons come from the Earth **orbiting** the Sun once a year, with a tilt.
- **"It's hottest on the longest day."** The longest day is about 21 June, but July and August are often warmer. The ground and sea take time to heat up, so the warmest weather lags behind the peak sunshine.

## Check your understanding

1. Is the Earth closest to the Sun in the UK's summer or winter?
2. What is the name of the imaginary line the Earth spins around, and how many degrees is it tilted?
3. Why does Australia have summer when we have winter?
4. Use the words **concentrated** and **spread out** to explain why sunlight in summer is stronger than in winter.
5. A friend says, "It's cold in December because we're further from the Sun." Write two sentences to show them why they're wrong.

### Answers

1. Winter (early January).
2. The axis, tilted by about 23.5 degrees.
3. When the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun, the Southern Hemisphere leans away, and the other way round six months later.
4. In summer, sunlight hits at a steep angle so it is concentrated onto a small area. In winter it arrives at a slant so it is spread out over a larger area.
5. Example: "The Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, so distance can't be the cause. It's cold because the UK is tilted away from the Sun, so the light is weaker and the days are shorter."

## One-line takeaway for the board

> **Tilt, not distance, gives us the seasons.**
