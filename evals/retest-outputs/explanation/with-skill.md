# Why we have seasons: Year 7 explanation

**Assumptions:** you have a torch (or a lamp with the shade off), a globe or a ball with a sticker on it for the UK, and a desk. Students have met the idea that Earth orbits the Sun and spins once a day. If you have no globe, a football and a marker pen work fine. Spoken time is about 3 minutes, plus the check questions.

---

## Teacher script

### Step 1. Start with their idea (20 seconds)

"Lots of people think summer happens because Earth swings closer to the Sun. That's a sensible guess. If you stand near a campfire you get hotter. So today we're going to test that idea and see if it survives."

**Check 1 (hands up, one word):** "If Earth being closer made it summer, what should be happening in Australia when it's summer here?"
- **Expected answer:** "The same. It should be summer there too," because the whole Earth would be closer at once.
- **Most likely wrong answer:** "Winter." Students who say this already know Australia is opposite. Praise it, then say: "Hold that thought, that fact is going to be the clue."

### Step 2. Concrete example first: the torch (45 seconds)

Hold the torch pointing **straight down** at the desk, about 20 cm away. Show the small, bright circle.

"Same torch, same battery, same brightness." Now tilt the torch so it hits the desk at a **slant**. The light stretches into a long, dim oval.

"Same amount of light. But now it's spread over a bigger patch, so each bit of desk gets less. Bright and small means strong. Stretched and dim means weak."

**Check 2:** "Which patch would warm up a piece of chocolate faster, and why?"
- **Expected answer:** The small, bright one, because the light is concentrated on a smaller area.
- **Most likely wrong answer:** "The slanted one, because it's bigger." Correct it: "Bigger yes, but the same energy shared out. Each square centimetre gets less."

### Step 3. Now the idea: Earth is tilted (60 seconds)

"The Sun's light hits Earth in exactly the same way. Where sunlight hits **straight on**, it's concentrated and warms the ground a lot. Where it hits at a **slant**, it's spread out and warms it less."

Hold up the globe. Point to the sticker for the UK.

"Earth's axis, the imaginary line it spins around, is **tilted by about 23 degrees**. It doesn't spin upright."

Put the lamp in the middle of the desk as the Sun. Hold the globe with the tilt pointing at a fixed spot on the back wall.

"In June, the top half of the Earth, the Northern Hemisphere, is leaning **towards** the Sun. The UK gets sunlight at a steep angle, high in the sky. The light is concentrated, and the days are long, about 16 hours in London."

Walk the globe halfway round the lamp, **keeping the tilt pointing at the same spot on the wall**.

"Six months later, the Earth is on the other side of the Sun. The tilt hasn't changed. It still points at the same spot. But now the Northern Hemisphere is leaning **away**. The sunlight arrives at a low slant, so it's spread out, and the days are short, about 8 hours in London. That's winter."

**Check 3:** "In December, is the UK tilted towards or away from the Sun? Which would give a more spread-out light?"
- **Expected answer:** Away from the Sun; the light is at a slant, so it's more spread out.
- **Most likely wrong answer:** "The Earth tilts back and forth during the year." Correct it: "The tilt direction stays fixed. It's the Earth's position in its orbit that changes which half is leaning towards the Sun."

### Step 4. Go back to their first idea (30 seconds)

"Now back to Australia. When the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun, the Southern Hemisphere leans away. So Australia has winter in our summer, and summer at Christmas. If distance caused the seasons, both would have the same season at the same time. They don't, so distance can't be the cause."

"And here's the surprise. Earth is actually **closest** to the Sun in early **January**, in the middle of our winter. The distance changes from about 147 million km in January to 152 million km in July. That's only about 3 per cent different, which is far too small to matter."

**Check 4:** "Give me one piece of evidence that the 'closer to the Sun' idea is wrong."
- **Expected answer:** Either (a) Australia has summer when we have winter, or (b) Earth is closest to the Sun in January, which is our winter.
- **Most likely wrong answer:** "Because Earth is too far away to feel the Sun." Redirect: "It does feel it, otherwise nothing would be warm. The point is that the small change in distance isn't what makes summer."

## The analogy, and where it breaks

**Analogy:** The torch on the desk. Straight on, the light is concentrated and strong. At a slant, it's spread out and weak. That's the Sun's light on Earth in summer and winter.

**Where it breaks:**
- **A torch is not the Sun.** Sunlight spreads out in all directions, and the torch is just a way of seeing the angle effect.
- **It only shows the angle, not the length of the day.** In summer, the days are also longer, so the ground has more hours to warm up. Say this out loud, because it's the second half of the story.
- **A desk is flat, but Earth is a ball.** On Earth, the angle changes gradually from the equator to the poles, which is why the Equator is hot all year and the poles are cold.

## Non-example (add after Step 3 if time allows)

**A campfire.** The closer you stand, the hotter you get. That's exactly why the "closer" idea feels right. But a campfire is a small, nearby heat source, and moving a few steps changes the distance a lot. Earth's distance to the Sun changes by only 3 per cent, so the *angle* of the light matters far more than the distance.

## Second example (shows the idea transfers)

**Noon sun in your own sky.** Ask students to think about their shadow at lunchtime. In summer it's short, because the Sun is high. In winter it's long, because the Sun is low. Short shadow means steep light, which means strong. Long shadow means slanted light, which means weak. It's the torch experiment happening outside.

## Key vocabulary (define it the first time you say it)

- **Axis:** the imaginary line through the North and South Poles that Earth spins around.
- **Axial tilt:** the angle the axis leans, about 23 degrees.
- **Orbit:** the path Earth takes around the Sun, which takes one year.
- **Hemisphere:** half of the Earth. The Northern Hemisphere is the top half.

## Most likely misconceptions to listen for

1. **"Summer is when Earth is closer to the Sun."** The main one. Pre-empted in Steps 1 and 4.
2. **"The Earth tilts towards and away from the Sun."** It tilts at a fixed angle in a fixed direction. Pre-empted in Step 3.
3. **"The Southern Hemisphere is closer in their summer."** Same distance idea, same fix: December is when Earth is closest to the Sun, yet Australia is hot and the UK is cold.

---

## One-paragraph version for students to read later

Earth has seasons because it is **tilted**, not because it gets closer to the Sun. Earth's axis leans by about 23 degrees, and it keeps leaning in the same direction as it travels around the Sun for a year. When the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun, sunlight hits it at a steep angle, so the light is concentrated and the days are long. That is summer. Six months later, the Northern Hemisphere leans away, so sunlight hits at a low slant and gets spread out, and the days are short. That is winter. At the same time, the Southern Hemisphere has the opposite season. This proves distance isn't the cause. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, in the middle of our winter, and the distance only changes by about 3 per cent over the year.

## Exit question (end of lesson, written)

"A friend says: 'It's summer because Earth moves closer to the Sun.' Write two sentences to show them why they're wrong."

- **Strong answer:** Mentions the tilt (or angle of sunlight) **and** one piece of evidence (Australia's opposite season, or Earth being closest in January).
- **Partial answer:** Says "it's the tilt" with no reason or evidence. Ask: "How does the tilt change the weather?"
